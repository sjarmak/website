OpenAI's contribution to the day was a safety post, and a substantive one, titled Safety and alignment in an era of long-horizon models. The subject is what changes when models stop running for minutes and start running for hours or days. The core claim, which Noam Brown amplified, is that persistence itself creates safety risks that shorter-horizon evaluations simply miss. A model that keeps working at an open-ended problem can drift in ways a twenty-minute eval never surfaces, and OpenAI says the lessons from studying one long-running model are reshaping its evaluations, its alignment work, its monitoring, and the controls it gives users. The post is thin on specifics about which failures they observed, which is frustrating and also typical of the genre, but the direction matters: the flagship labs are conceding that their existing evaluation stacks were built for a model lifetime measured in minutes, and the products they are all shipping no longer live in that regime. Put this next to the Hugging Face story and the shape of the moment is hard to miss. The long-horizon agent showed up in the same news cycle as both a safety subject and a security threat, the thing the labs are trying to align and the thing that just breached a major AI company.

One more thread, on whether you can trust benchmark numbers at all. swyx flagged a writeup buried in the RLM paper from Alex Zhang and Omar Khattab, and his framing of the open secret is worth repeating. Even without training on a benchmark's test set, a lab can goalseek nearly any number it wants by training on lookalikes of the test, tasks built to resemble the benchmark closely enough that the score stops measuring generalization. And because open-weight releases almost never include the training datasets or the RL environments, nobody outside the lab can check, which gives everyone plausible deniability. The RLM authors take a first swing at detection, applying standard NLP distance metrics to hidden trajectories, and they claim no full solution. But their analysis does support something encouraging: the recursive language models in the paper genuinely generalize to unseen tasks that share latent structure with what they trained on, which is exactly the kind of claim the field needs better tools to verify.
