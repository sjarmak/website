Researchers at Noma published a writeup of something they've named GitLost, which is a working attack that tricks GitHub's AI agent into leaking private repositories. Through injected instructions, they got the agent to exfiltrate private code. And the reason this matters more than the average vulnerability disclosure is precisely that it's concrete and it works. It is not, here's a theoretical risk if an agent were somehow compromised. It's, here is a chain that works, here is private data actually coming out the other end. Prompt injection has been the theoretical boogeyman of the whole agent era for a while now, the thing everyone gestures at nervously, and this is one of the clearer public demonstrations that the boogeyman has hands and can pick the lock.

And GitLost lands right next to a piece of research from VentureBeat that quantifies why this class of thing is so dangerous at scale. They found that shared API keys expose AI agent fleets at sixty-nine percent of enterprises. Sixty-nine percent. So the substantial majority of companies deploying these agents are doing it with shared credentials, which means the moment any single agent in the fleet gets compromised, through exactly a GitLost-style injection, the blast radius isn't one agent and its little corner. It's everything that shared key can touch. You've now got a demonstrated exfiltration technique on one side, and on the other side an environment where most organizations have wired their agents up to fail catastrophically instead of gracefully, in the open, where they'd see it if they looked, and they mostly aren't looking. That is not a comfortable combination, and it's the kind of thing that stays invisible right up until it's the subject of an incident report and a very bad week.

On the defensive side, though, there was at least one genuinely encouraging signal. GitHub shipped CodeQL 2.26.0, and alongside adding support for Kotlin 2.4.0, it added AI prompt-injection detection. Now, CodeQL is the static-analysis engine behind GitHub's code scanning, it's the thing that flags SQL injection and the classic vulnerability classes across your codebase on every push. And now prompt injection is a first-class citizen inside that same scanner. I think that's a small moment that's actually worth marking and remembering. When a vulnerability class graduates into the standard static-analysis tooling that runs automatically on everyone's commits, it means the industry has collectively decided that this is a real, permanent, catalogued category of bug, not a novel curiosity you read about in a blog post. Prompt injection just joined SQL injection in the canon of things your scanner watches for. That's maturation. It's the uncomfortable kind of maturation, the kind that only happens because the threat turned out to be real, but it's maturation, and it's the right direction.
