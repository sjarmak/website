A couple more data points, because some of them are genuinely striking. On ARC-AGI-2, the abstraction-and-reasoning benchmark that's been a real stumbling block for these models, Sol scored 92.5%, which one of the evaluators called state of the art at an order of magnitude less cost than GPT-5.5 Pro just three months earlier. Same family, three months, roughly a tenth the cost for a better score. That pace is the actual news. On ARC-AGI-3, the newer, harder, game-based version, Sol became the first verified frontier model to ever actually beat one of the games. Now, it only scored 7.8% overall, so let's be honest, nobody has solved ARC-AGI-3, it remains brutal. But going from zero models ever beating a single game to one model beating a game is the kind of threshold crossing that tends to look important in hindsight. There were also day-zero results on ParseBench, the document-parsing benchmark, and those were more mixed. GPT-5.6 continues to do well on plain text and tables, but it still struggles on charts and complex layout, with persistent weakness on things like chart transcription and figuring out where elements sit on a page. And interestingly, Luna came in about six times cheaper than Sol on that benchmark with only minor degradation, which again points you toward the small models for high-volume, forgiving document work. On the other end of the difficulty spectrum, on a benchmark called CritPt, which is built from unpublished, research-level physics problems, Sol at max led by roughly four points over Fable 5. So it's genuinely strong at the very hardest reasoning, and genuinely still weak at reading a messy PDF. Both things are true, and the gap between them is the honest shape of these models right now.

And there was one demonstration that got passed around a lot, from the very top of the lineup. OpenAI made GPT-5.6 Sol Ultra generally available, and then shared that it had produced a proof of the fifty-year-old Cycle Double Cover Conjecture, a real open problem in graph theory, using sixty-four subagents working in parallel in just under an hour. Now, take that with the appropriate grain of salt, extraordinary claims and all that, the proof needs the mathematics community to actually verify it. But set the verification question aside for a second and look at the shape of the claim. Sixty-four subagents, orchestrated in parallel, chewing on a hard math problem for an hour. That's not a chatbot answering a question. That's a small automated research team. Whether or not this specific proof holds, the workflow it describes is the thing to pay attention to, because that's clearly where OpenAI thinks the ceiling use case is heading.

One more thing on the model before the product, and it's the safety framing, because it connects to that CyberBench point. OpenAI said GPT-5.6 is its most capable model yet on cyber and bio-related tasks, and in the same breath said that some API calls in those dual-use areas may be blocked or paused for extra safety review. So the same capability that lets Sol top CyberBench is capability OpenAI is nervous enough about that they're gating access to parts of it. That tension, most capable yet on cyber and bio, and therefore partially fenced off, is going to be a recurring feature of every frontier release from here. The models get more useful for offense and defense at the same time, and the labs are increasingly the ones deciding, at the API layer, which side of that you get to be on.
