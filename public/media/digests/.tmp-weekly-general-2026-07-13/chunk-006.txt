The sharpest single statement of it came from a piece on the Octopus blog, bluntly titled, code review is theater now. And the argument lands hard. When a large fraction of the diff sitting in front of you was written by a machine, and you, the human reviewer, are skimming a few hundred lines looking for anything obviously wrong before you click approve, the ritual of review is still happening, but the assurance it was designed to provide has drained out of it. Review was invented for a world where a human wrote the code, reasoning their way through it line by line, and another human checked that reasoning. When the author is instead a model that produces plausible, well-structured, confidently-wrong code faster than you can even read it, the human check simply doesn't scale to match, and pretending it does is the theater the title is pointing at. Now, I don't think the honest conclusion is stop reviewing. But I think the piece is exactly right that a lot of teams are still performing the old ritual over a fundamentally changed reality, and the gap between the performance of assurance and the actual protection is widening fast, in plain sight, and most orgs haven't adjusted their process to it at all.

George Hotz added a useful counterweight to the week's wall-to-wall hype with a post called, I love LLMs, I hate hype, which does exactly what the title promises. He separates the genuine, real, load-bearing capability of these models, which he clearly respects, from the marketing froth wrapped around them, which he clearly doesn't. And coming from someone with his engineering credibility, it's a healthy corrective in a week that was almost nothing but launch announcements. The valuable move in it is the reminder that you can be genuinely, deeply bullish on the underlying technology and still find the hype cycle exhausting and dishonest. Those two positions aren't in tension. It's good to hear someone that technical say it that plainly.

Gergely Orosz spent a good chunk of the week poking at a term that's been making the rounds, loop engineering, and he was refreshingly, productively skeptical. He said he's been looking into it, experimenting himself, and he's not yet buying that it's a genuinely new paradigm as opposed to a fresh name for things we already do. And when he asked people what loops they actually run in practice, the answers came back as, one, agents kicked off when an event triggers, which some folks are calling events engineering, and two, periodic work, nightly runs, scheduled jobs. Which, as he dryly points out, is cron and event handlers with a language model sitting in the loop. Now, I'll push back on him a little, because sometimes a new name for an existing pattern is genuinely useful, it focuses collective attention and it gives people a thing to search for and standardize around. But I think his default instinct is the correct one. Be suspicious when the industry invents a grand new engineering discipline for what turns out, on inspection, to be a shell script on a timer. The burden of proof is on the person claiming the paradigm shift, not on the skeptic, and most of the time that burden doesn't get met.

If you want the substantive, unhurried, deeply skeptical version of all this discourse, the best long read of the week by a distance was Dan Luu's notes on agentic coding, which clocks in at something like ninety-two minutes of reading. He works through agentic test processes, LLM benchmarks, the reality behind the vendor claims, all of it with the kind of careful, evidence-first skepticism that this topic almost never gets. It is the direct antidote to the launch-hype cycle we just spent twenty minutes talking about. If you only take one thing off this entire episode that isn't a product you have to evaluate for your job, make it that piece. Block out the time, it's worth it.
