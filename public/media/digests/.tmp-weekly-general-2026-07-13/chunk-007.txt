And here is where the craft question stops being philosophical and starts landing directly in people's inboxes. There was a widely-read thread on the Software Engineering subreddit titled, simply and wearily, I don't want your PRs anymore. And it is the maintainer's cry from the heart for this exact moment. Open-source maintainers, and increasingly internal ones too, are drowning in AI-generated pull requests. Plausible-looking, well-formatted, superficially reasonable contributions that cost far more time to properly review and reject than they would ever have saved if they'd been good enough to merge. The volume of contributions went way, way up. The value did not track the volume at all. And this is the shadow side of the cheerful story that anyone can now generate a pull request in thirty seconds. The bottleneck just moves. It moves to the human being who has to figure out whether the thirty-second PR is a real contribution or confident garbage, and that human being is getting absolutely crushed under the load. I think this is going to be one of the defining social problems of open source over the next year, and I don't think anyone has a good answer to it yet. Rate limits on contributions? Reputation systems? AI reviewers screening the AI-written PRs, which is just the review theater we were talking about wearing a different hat? Whatever the answer is, the current equilibrium is not stable, and the maintainers saying so out loud this week are the early-warning system for a problem that's about to be a lot of people's problem.

And there's a related shift happening one level down, in the shape of the code itself, not just the process around it. A thoughtful thread on the Software Engineering subreddit this week, titled The Vertical Codebase, argued that AI is changing how codebases get structured, not only how fast they get written. The claim, roughly, is that when generation is cheap and an agent can hold and regenerate a large chunk of code in one shot, you start optimizing the codebase for the agent's comprehension rather than the human's, and that pushes you toward different structures than the ones that decades of human-oriented style guides gave us. You can see the concrete, almost comical version of it in another post that went around this week, where a developer had Codex split a single five-thousand-two-hundred-line React component into eighty-five separate files, and then reported that the refactor itself was the easy part, the boring part, next to actually making the underlying feature reliable. Think about that pairing for a second. The mechanical work of decomposition, breaking a giant file into a clean tree of small ones, which used to be a dreaded multi-day slog that people avoided precisely because it was so tedious and risky, has become nearly free. And when a formerly expensive thing becomes free, the interesting questions don't disappear, they move up a level. They move to what the right decomposition even is, and to who, or what, the structure is actually for. If your files are increasingly organized for an agent to navigate and regenerate rather than for a human to read top to bottom, then the definition of good code, the thing every senior engineer thought they knew in their bones, is now openly up for renegotiation, and I don't think the industry has begun to agree on the answer. Do you write for the human who has to trust the code, or for the agent who has to change it? Those two readers want different things, and for the first time you actually have to choose. I suspect a lot of the style arguments of the next few years are going to be some version of that question wearing different clothes.
