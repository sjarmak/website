New Relic put out its 2026 State of AI Coding Report, which is the year's big survey-scale snapshot of where adoption actually is and, more usefully, where it's actually landing in production versus where the marketing says it's landing. These annual reports are worth reading not for any single headline percentage but for the delta, year over year, the shape of what's genuinely changing underneath the noise. Pair that report with something Databricks did that I liked a lot as a pure methodology point. They benchmarked coding agents against their own internal engineering tasks, their real work, rather than against the public leaderboards. And that, right there, is the move. Public benchmarks are increasingly contaminated, increasingly gamed, and increasingly disconnected from the actual work your team does. The companies getting real, trustworthy signal about these tools are the ones building private evals on their own tasks, tasks that resist contamination for the simple reason that they have never been on the public internet for a model to have trained on. If you take one single operational lesson from this whole episode, let it be that one. Stop trusting the leaderboard. Build the eval that reflects your actual codebase. The Databricks writeup is a good, concrete template for how to start.

And finally, away from all the coding and the launches and the pricing wars, the segment that genuinely stuck with me most. Anthropic's interpretability team published research suggesting that Claude maintains something like a readable global workspace. The idea, roughly stated, is that there are internal concepts the model is actively tracking, and that you can read those concepts before they show up in the model's actual output. Now, think for a second about what that would mean if it holds up and if it generalizes. Today, we almost entirely evaluate a model by what it says. We wait for the output, and then we judge the output. A readable internal workspace points toward a different world, one where you can see what the model is about to do, what concept it's holding onto, before it acts on that concept. And for reliability and for safety, that's close to the whole ballgame. Catching a problem in the model's intent, for lack of a better word, rather than catching it in the output after the fact when it might already be too late. Now, I want to be measured here. It's early research, and interpretability as a field has a long history of results that look absolutely profound in the paper and turn out narrow or fragile in practice. So don't oversell it in your own head. But of everything that happened this dense, loud week, that one piece of research is the one I'd bet has the longest half-life.
