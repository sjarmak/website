So he decoupled it. Whatever writes the code is not allowed to validate it. The validation step routes to a completely isolated model instance with no prior conversation context and no exposure to the generation prompt, and its only job is to play devil's advocate, inspect the code, and challenge assumptions. He says it eliminated the self-rubber-stamped logic bugs overnight, and he's honest that it does nothing for open-ended architectural questions. His closing question is the one I'd like to see answered: how do you force a verifier to actually execute the tests and inspect runtime output, rather than reading the diff statically and pronouncing it fine? Because a verifier that only reads is still guessing, and the pagination bug in his story was invisible in the diff and obvious in a run with two pages of data.

Two more things worth your time, both on the retrieval side.

SAGE is an adaptive retrieval policy for RAG systems running under service level objectives, and it's the most immediately usable result of the day. The observation is simple. Fixed top-k budgets over-retrieve for easy queries and under-serve hard ones, so you end up trading answer quality against tail latency at the pipeline level, for every query, regardless of what the query needs. SAGE learns a policy that picks k per query using features you already have after the initial retrieval: score distributions, rank gaps, lexical signals. It's trained offline by imitation learning against an oracle that approximates the optimal latency-quality trade-off, and at inference it adds no LLM calls and minimal overhead. Under a five-second P95 latency target on Natural Questions, it hits ninety-five percent SLO compliance against thirty percent for the best static baseline at k equals twenty. P95 latency drops thirty-six percent, retrieval cost drops fifty-one percent, and the cost is two points of exact match. A single policy trained on Natural Questions generalizes to HotpotQA and UnSeenTimeQA and across four model families, Llama, Qwen, Mistral, and Gemma, holding forty-five to fifty-two points of SLO improvement without quality degradation. If you operate a production RAG pipeline with a fixed k, that's a straightforward thing to go try.

The last one is a position paper called Muscle Memory, and I like the framing even though the evidence is thin. The argument is that agent memory has converged on one architecture: store experience as text or embeddings or reflections or rules, retrieve it at inference time, and let a general-purpose orchestrator interpret what to do with it. The authors argue that's the wrong default for personalization, and that recurring user intent should be compiled into purpose-built specialist agents instead. Their pipeline harvests conversation history, separates behavioral patterns from task patterns, augments, and evaluates, emitting quality-gated executable specialists with two-stage trigger matching. On ninety held-out scenarios across five personas, the augmented assistant wins thirty-two of the thirty-six cases where a specialist actually fires, an eighty-nine percent win rate, with a small personalization gain and a small accuracy cost. Small sample, and it's a position paper. But the idea that some of what we retrieve at inference is stable enough to compile ahead of time is worth carrying around.

What connects all of this is that in every case the model was capable of the work, and the structure around it decided the outcome. The topology, the retrieval budget, the scope written down before the agent started, the verifier that has never seen the generation context. The thing I'll be watching is whether SWE-RPG's intermediate ground truth gets adopted by people building agents rather than just people measuring them. A benchmark that tells you your agent lost the requirement at minute three is worth considerably more than one that tells you the patch failed. And I'm still waiting for the first verifier that runs the code.
