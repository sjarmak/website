Sarah Stamps studies a continent in motion. Her research is especially associated with the East African Rift, where the crust is stretching and the future shape of a plate boundary is still being made. The field becomes legible when several kinds of evidence are placed on the same map: GNSS velocities, radar interferometry, earthquakes, geological structures, and models of the lithosphere.

The first challenge is reference. A station moves only relative to something else. Global plate-motion models provide a frame, while regional networks resolve the small velocities that distinguish one rift branch from another. Those velocities are not just arrows. They encode how extension is partitioned, where strain concentrates, and whether a block behaves as a coherent unit.

Rifting is rarely uniform. Faults, volcanic zones, inherited weaknesses, and deep lithospheric structure all influence localization. A kinematic block model can summarize the motion, but it does not by itself explain the force or material that produces it. Geodynamic models supply that mechanical layer. They test whether the observed pattern is consistent with thermal structure, buoyancy, plate boundary forces, or inherited architecture.

Earthquakes add another window. In a slowly deforming continent, seismicity can be sparse, but each event helps locate active faults and estimate how strain is released. Geodesy sees gradual motion; seismology sees sudden failure. Their combination connects the earthquake cycle to the longer evolution of the rift.

InSAR changes the spatial scale. Radar can reveal deformation between GNSS stations, including signals around faults and volcanoes. It also introduces its own problems: atmospheric delay, decorrelation, orbital errors, and choices about processing. A dense image is not automatically a better constraint. Its uncertainty and reference must be carried into the inversion.

The research progression therefore runs from measuring motion to asking what motion means. Competing models can fit a handful of velocities. Independent sensors and longer time series make the comparison harder and more useful. A model of breakup should explain present deformation while remaining compatible with earthquakes, topography, geology, and the depth distribution of strain.

The frontier is witnessing a transition. Will extension remain distributed across broad zones, or will it focus into a narrow plate boundary? What observation would distinguish those paths before the geological record is complete? Which combination of GNSS, radar, seismicity, and gravity can expose the responsible structure below the surface?

Time is the constraint that ties these measurements together. A campaign can reveal a velocity field, but a continuous station can reveal whether that field changes after an earthquake, a volcanic intrusion, or a seasonal hydrologic cycle. Long records separate tectonic motion from temporary signals. They also make instrument maintenance and processing history part of the scientific archive.

Models should preserve disagreement. A block model, an elastic dislocation model, and a viscoelastic geodynamic model may all fit the first dataset. Their predictions can diverge when a new station is added or when a seismic sequence occurs. Designing observations around those divergences is more informative than collecting measurements without a model comparison in mind.

The public value is clearest where tectonic science meets hazard. A better estimate of strain does not predict an earthquake on a particular day. It can identify active structures, improve scenario planning, and show where monitoring is too sparse to support a confident conclusion. Precision is useful when its limits remain visible.

Stamps’s field is a reminder that geodesy is not simply about precise coordinates. It is about building a test between competing explanations of how continents deform. The most valuable observing system is one that preserves small signals over decades and makes its reference frames, processing choices, and uncertainties reproducible. That is how a moving continent becomes a scientific argument rather than a collection of maps.

The reading path begins with the rift as a geological system. Continental breakup is not a single crack appearing in an intact plate. Extension may spread across faults and volcanic zones, while inherited structures guide where strain concentrates. The Red Sea and Afar provide a natural comparison because extension, magmatism, and lithospheric thinning occur together but not in one simple geometry.

Geodesy changes the question from where a rift is to how it is moving now. A GNSS station records a position relative to a reference frame. A network turns those positions into velocities. A regional model turns velocities into blocks or strain rates. Each step adds interpretation. A velocity field is valuable precisely because its frame, time span, and uncertainty are explicit.

The East African Rift is a demanding setting for this work. Stations are unevenly distributed, earthquakes are episodic, and volcanic or hydrologic signals can overlap tectonic motion. A short campaign may capture a transient. A long time series can separate persistent extension from temporary deformation, but only if station histories and processing changes are preserved.

Strain-rate models provide a bridge between measurements and mechanics. They describe how extension is distributed, but they do not by themselves identify the force that drives it. A plume, plate-boundary traction, inherited weakness, or changes in lithospheric strength can produce similar surface patterns. The model comparison becomes stronger when it uses earthquakes, geology, topography, and mantle structure as independent constraints.

Earthquakes reveal where the continent fails abruptly. A large rupture redistributes stress across nearby fault segments, and that redistribution can influence later activity. The relationship is not a timetable for earthquakes. It is evidence about how faults interact mechanically. Intrarift interactions are therefore part of the long-term rift story as well as a short-term hazard question.

The geological record provides the longer view. Rift lakes, volcanic products, and exposed structures preserve evidence of subsidence, extension, and changing drainage. Those records are difficult to align with modern velocities because they integrate different time spans. Their value is that they test whether a present-day model can plausibly produce the observed history.

The field’s instruments also have different failure modes. GNSS can suffer monument motion or reference errors. Radar interferometry can be disrupted by atmosphere and decorrelation. Seismic catalogs depend on network completeness. Geological maps reflect exposure and interpretation. A multi-sensor model should carry those limitations rather than treating each layer as equally certain.

This makes open data practice central. A useful deformation product should publish coordinates, reference frame, time interval, processing decisions, and quality flags. A strain model should provide its observations and parameterization. A geodynamic model should state its rheology and boundary conditions. Without those details, independent groups can compare pictures but cannot test explanations.

The frontier is the transition from distributed extension to a recognizable plate boundary. That transition may take millions of years, yet the decisive signals can be small: a persistent change in velocity gradients, a new concentration of seismicity, or a shift in magmatic alignment. Detecting such signals requires observatories that are stable enough to preserve them and flexible enough to incorporate new sensors.

The practical lesson is that precision alone does not settle a tectonic question. A model becomes persuasive when different measurements converge on the same mechanism and when a competing model makes a prediction that can be checked. Sarah Stamps’s research sits in that space between measurement and explanation, using geodesy to make continental deformation visible while keeping the uncertainty that gives the measurement scientific meaning.

That approach also changes how observations are planned. The best next station is not always the one that fills the largest geographic gap. It may be the one placed where two models predict different velocities, or where a seismic sequence would most clearly test stress transfer. Observation becomes an experiment designed around disagreement. Such experiments are especially valuable in a rift, where many mechanisms can explain the first broad pattern of extension.

The same principle applies to time. A new instrument can reveal a striking signal, but a durable network can show whether the signal persists, repeats, or disappears. Instrument metadata, monument surveys, antenna changes, radar processing versions, and earthquake catalog revisions are therefore part of the tectonic archive. Without them, a change in the time series can be mistaken for a change in the Earth.

Rift science has public consequences even when it cannot predict an earthquake date. Better maps of active faults and strain help prioritize monitoring and preparedness. Better understanding of volcanic and seismic interactions improves scenario planning. Clear uncertainty tells communities where the evidence is strong and where decisions must remain precautionary. The scientific value and the civic value meet when a deformation model is transparent enough to be questioned.

That is the work.
