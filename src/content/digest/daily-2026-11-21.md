Under OpenHands, five coding models averaged 87.69% on per-task scores in [JoyFrontBench](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv261114952Q), and only 51.52% of the applications they built actually worked. The benchmark writes a goal-oriented acceptance contract for each of its 100 front-end problems before generation starts: core tasks, mandatory criteria, a shared completion threshold. An application counts as a success only when every core task passes on the same artifact within one run. That rule separates "most of the pieces are there" from "the user can finish the workflow," and the 36-point gap between those two numbers is the size of the error you make when you report the first as if it were the second. The 764 tasks and 2,596 atomic criteria come from practitioner requirements, with evidence-grounded judgments backed by deterministic record auditing, and the authors also ran OpenCode as a second harness so the gap is not an artifact of one scaffold.

The same mismatch between partial credit and an end-to-end outcome shows up in agentic RAG, where the question is where a failure started. [AgenticRAG-FP](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.20627) (v2, November 13) injects a certified fault at a known hop, reruns the rest of the trajectory, and asks diagnosers to find the injected hop from the trace. Coverage-based diagnosis scores 0.91 at hop 1 and 0.00 at hops 2 and 3 on three-hop MuSiQue questions with Claude Haiku 4.5, because it guesses hop 1 for 96% of failures; that is an earliest-hop rule, not a diagnosis. An LLM judge and frozen-hop repair localize between 0.25 and 0.48 of the later-hop failures, and under content corruption at depth 2 they reach 0.49 and 0.59 exact-hop accuracy without a statistically significant difference between them (McNemar p=0.33). Any trace-debugging tool that reports high attribution accuracy on a dataset where most faults sit at the first hop deserves a second look.

Environment setup is where coding agents spend effort before they can do anything useful, and [HerAgent](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.18443) (November 13) argues the problem is granularity of recovery. Setup agents today checkpoint either per command, which is too local to clear polluted state, or per whole script, which throws away good progress on any failure. HerAgent's unit is a semantic setup block that is executed, validated, checkpointed, repaired, and rolled back as one thing. Against six prior agents on four datasets it wins every comparison, lifting success on Installamatic-Bench from 52.0% to 85.0% and on SetupBench from 46.3% to 70.4%, and cuts setup cost by up to 94.5%. The design is a direct application of durable-execution thinking to a shell session: pick the step boundary at which state is both meaningful and restorable.

[guardscope](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv261115223T) measures something enterprise agent gateways rarely state: which of an agent's actions they actually see. A control can only check actions that cross its placement point, and agents act through more paths than the tool-call channel. The framework predicts statically which of 15 action paths a control can observe, then runs a fixed workload on one of eight deployment topologies and classifies each executed action as checked, bypassed, or prevented by reconciling the control's decision records against kernel-level ground truth. The authors ran it against Microsoft's agent governance toolkit and agentgateway with two harnesses. The useful output is a protocol: a vendor can publish a kernel-verified coverage claim instead of a feature list.

On the enterprise-data side, [RetailBridge](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.25096) (v2, November 13) builds compositional questions that require chaining entities and intermediate answers across structured product and order records and unstructured inquiries and reviews. Its reference system, RetailRAG, adds auto-schema briefing, source-routed plan chaining, and diagnostic plan revision, and it beats existing agentic RAG baselines on every axis. Across ten LLM backbones, multi-source chaining is the dominant bottleneck regardless of provider or scale. That fits the semantic-governance argument from the retrieval side: the hard part is knowing which source holds the answer and carrying meaning between them, and a larger model does not supply that.

Two smaller items fill in the coding and orchestration picture. [Spec-Driven PBT](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.15920) (November 13) has an agent write invariants in natural language from the product spec before it reads the implementation, then translate them into property tests. Across 25 ViBench applications it finds bugs that code-derived properties miss, because properties derived from the implementation inherit its choices. [DecArena](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.15521) (November 13) removes the global orchestrator: agents see only local state, talk only to graph neighbours, and must discover tools among more than 100,000. Current LLM agents struggle there and RL alone does not fix it, while the GraphHarness baseline (topology-aware warmup plus self-improving routing) raises task success by 48.5 points at lower cost.

Both JoyFrontBench and guardscope report a ratio that vendors do not publish: core-task pass versus application success, and checked actions versus total actions. Watch whether either ratio starts appearing on leaderboards and gateway datasheets, or stays inside papers.
