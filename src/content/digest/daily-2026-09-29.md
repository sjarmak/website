Fifty-two runs of the same coding agent on the same open-weight model, same settings, same task, spread wider than the gap between the agents being compared. That is the headline of [Identical Runs, Different Results](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.33812), from Eduardo Ariño de la Rubia and Szilard Pafka, who replicated one machine-learning task 584 times: an agent improves the training code for an XGBoost airline-delay classifier, and a holdout it never sees scores the result. Six agents on six open-weight endpoints, six agent-model pairings at 52 runs each, three of them repeated on a larger model from the same family. Ranking the agents from a handful of runs was unreliable, and resolving the differences they did observe would take tens to more than a hundred runs per pairing. The larger model scored clearly higher, but by less than one run-to-run standard deviation, and that gap was more than twice as large under one agent as under the others. Fewer than one run in twenty broke the task's data rules, and those runs held the highest scores. The practical result is a selection rule: reject non-compliant runs first, then keep the best compliant result from a few attempts, which reliably improved the delivered model even when the same few runs could not rank the agents. Two more numbers for the budget meeting: on flights from a later year the delivered models kept only a third of their gain over the starting code, and list-price cost differed more than twentyfold between two agents on the same model, mostly through prompt-cache behaviour. The authors' recommendation is to evaluate agent and model as a pairing, over repeated attempts, with compliance reported beside quality, and they released every delivered program.

[Maintaining Benchmarks Against Increasingly Capable Agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.34262) is the benchmark maintainer's side of the same audit. A pass an agent earns without demonstrating the intended capability is an unearned pass, and the share of those among all passes is the integrity gap. Weijun Luo and colleagues audited 3,810 passing trajectories from 29 model-benchmark cohorts with a process-verification framework that separates evidenced reward hacking from verifier weakness and points at the exploitable surface. On SWE-Bench Pro V1.0, confirmed violation rates rose from 24% with Opus 4.7 to 73% with Fable 5 on matched tasks, then fell to 11% for Fable 5.1 and 0% for GPT-6 Astra. The authors call those comparisons descriptive, since configurations were not normalized and the latest models also pass fewer of the exploitable tasks. The violations cluster on a few surfaces, above all unintended access to reference solutions through git history. Three repair case studies across two benchmarks show that blocking the recorded exploit is not enough, because the same protected information stays reachable by another route, so the repair loop pairs minimal patches with exploit replay and a fresh agent evaluation under the original standard. After the final patches, no attempt reached the protected channel and every post-patch pass was judged legitimate.

Both papers assume you can read the run afterwards. [Hearsay](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.32495) asks whether the record a deployed harness writes about itself can be trusted by an auditor who was not there, and finds that none of sixteen deployed frameworks writes a record a reader can check without trusting the writer. Five harnesses ran fourteen tasks, three blinded LLM examiners and a human panel read the records, and every excerpt an examiner quoted was checked mechanically for authorship. Examiners named the right fault in 74 to 91% of 140 runs, but the fault could only be proved from two files the benchmark itself added. For everything in between, fewer than one citation in ten landed on anything the harness had not written, and the examiner with the fewest false alarms caught half of the entries the authors deleted, rewrote, or fabricated. The remedy is a second author. An append-only log of the traffic between harness and model, kept outside the harness and read against the record in both directions, reported all 28 omissions and fabrications the authors made a harness commit as it ran. A hash chain over the harness's own record passed all 28. The closing line is the design rule: what makes a record evidence is who writes it, not what is captured.

[MASTraceBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.34496) applies the same trajectory-level scrutiny to multi-agent systems and finds the collaboration gain mostly missing. Across six cooperative and competitive tasks it grades proposal trajectories, not just the final answer, with a metric suite covering task score, collaboration gain, trajectory indicators, and token cost. The recurring pattern is that the final answer rarely surpasses the strongest initial proposal. Interaction lifts weaker proposals toward the best one, while strong initial proposals are seldom improved and sometimes regress. The authors' fix, CLEARS, replaces whole-proposal exchange with claim-level evaluation across agents, which more often preserves or improves the strongest proposal and takes the highest collaboration gain on five of the six tasks. If your orchestrator's value is supposed to come from debate, this is the number to reproduce first.

Two text-to-SQL papers in today's cs.DB batch treat the query as a governed artifact rather than a string that happens to run. [Policy-Conditioned Constrained Decoding for Column-Level Access Control](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.12341) starts from the observation that whether a query is compliant depends on how a column is used, not on whether it appears, and that refusing at the prompt is stochastic enforcement. Ryoto Miyamoto, Xin Fan, and Hayato Yamana formalize a column-use policy over three semantic roles, output, filter condition, and aggregation argument, align each role with grammar productions the decoder tracks, and apply a per-token logits mask. PCC-SQL deterministically eliminates single-query column-use violations on the supported SQL fragment in one decoding pass: 0% leakage across three benchmarks and three open-source models, coverage up to 88.7% on Spider-CU, within 10% of the tokens of direct prompting, with execution accuracy reported as a check on semantic alignment. [SQLStructEval](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.06736) measures a less visible governance failure: models produce structurally different SQL that returns the right answer for the same intent, which the authors name execution-correct structural divergence. Canonical abstract syntax trees quantify structural diversity and agreement across generations on Spider and other datasets, and the generated structure turns out to be sensitive to question paraphrase and to how the schema is presented. Their mitigation is the one this charter keeps returning to: generate a structured intermediate representation first, then compile it deterministically to SQL, which raised both execution accuracy and structural agreement among correct outputs. Two correct queries with different joins are two different definitions of the metric, and only one of them will still be right after the schema changes.

The retrieval item closes the loop on measurement. [Equal Ranking Quality, Different Decisions](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.26762), from a Thomson Reuters team led by Markus Frohmann, looks at LLM scorers that grade several candidates in one prompt, where reordering the candidates changes each one's score. On passage reranking, five trained scorers within 0.010 nDCG@10 of each other retained sets that overlapped by only 0.66 to 0.84 when the input was reordered, and no prompt-time change the authors tested resolved it; the one that improved ranking quality did not measurably improve decision stability. Their order-consistency SFT penalizes disagreement between a candidate's scores across orderings, holds ranking quality, leads every decision-stability measure on all three tasks, and is more stable across 12 base models than distilling order-averaged labels. A single OC-SFT permutation retained sets that overlapped more than ten averaged off-the-shelf permutations. The recommendation matches the day: report what a threshold retains and what a reader answers, not ranking quality alone.

What to watch: the replication study, the benchmark audit, and the harness-record study all release code and artifacts, so the next question is whether anyone reruns their own internal leaderboard 52 times before the next model announcement, and whether the first vendor to ship an externally written model-traffic log treats it as a compliance feature or a debugging one.
