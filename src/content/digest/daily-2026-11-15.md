In 11 of 2,160 tau2-bench episodes, an agent executed a database write its policy forbade, and an LLM judge passed 10 of those 11. Give the judge the policy document and it still passes 10; swap in a stronger judge and it passes 6. A rule-based check with no model call, which compares whether a write was expected against whether one appears in the trace, caught all 11. That result comes from [Sungbin Im's study of evaluator disagreement](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.14581), and it frames most of what landed in the last day: agents that are judged by the wrong instrument, stop at the wrong time, or settle on the wrong answer for reasons they find acceptable.

The tau2-bench paper deserves a careful read if you run agents that write to systems of record. Im scored every episode four ways (native gold-state scoring, an LLM judge, the judge plus policy, and the boundary evaluator) and had two annotators label 120 episodes. The judge was self-consistent, repeating its verdict 98.2% of the time, yet agreed with native scoring on only 56.2%. Consistency is not validity. The second finding is worse for anyone shipping guardrails: forbidden writes occurred in 11.1% of ungated episodes, and verification, correction, and safe-termination gates did not reduce them significantly. The traces point to a mundane cause; the gates' evidence digest dropped order status and customer authentication, so the gates rejected valid requests instead of catching bad ones. Commit boundaries are where the money is, and a cheap deterministic check at that boundary beat every judge configuration on human labels.

[Termination Lag](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.14960), from Cim, Topcu, and Kandemir, measures a different waste. They recorded and tested every distinct patch across five models, two agent frameworks, and three SWE-bench datasets. Between 38% and 82% of successful runs had a correct solution on their first source edit. The agents kept going anyway: post-solution steps were a median 35–37% of successful runs for Qwen3-Coder and 54–60% for Laguna-XS.2 on Verified and Multilingual, mostly spent verifying and exploring. In 1–8% of cases that extra activity undid the correct patch. If you pay per token, a third to half of a successful run is spend after the answer exists, and the agent has no reliable signal that it is done. That lines up with the argument in Jeff An's Stack Overflow post, [Your coding agent shouldn't grade its own homework](https://stackoverflow.blog/2026/11/13/your-coding-agent-shouldn-t-grade-its-own-homework/): self-verification is the step agents are worst at, and the termination data shows it is also where they burn the most steps.

Ruixin Wang and Tianyi Zhang name a related failure in [Eliciting and Mitigating Specification Satisficing in Coding Agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.16289). The agent considers an implementation that meets a requirement, then commits to one that does not, for a reason it judges acceptable. Standard self-correction inherits that bias, because the agent already approved the choice. Their fix, SatisFix, has the agent list such choices and its reasons, then checks each against the task description. Across five backbone LLMs and two frameworks it lifts pass@1 by an average 5.09% on DeepSWE and 7.06% on the hard subset of SWE-bench Pro, beating the best self-correction baseline on DeepSWE by 3.98%. The mechanism matters more than the margin: it moves the check from "does this look right to me" to "does this match the spec as written."

[ArcticSwarm](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.01870) applies the same instinct to multi-agent research, where no verifier exists. Agents that read a shared board adopt an early candidate before alternatives are tested; the authors call this early consensus. ArcticSwarm separates gathering from integration: a gathering gate lets selected search tasks post findings without reading the board, and an integration gate checks a candidate at three steps and reopens search on failure rather than just rejecting. On all 830 BrowseComp-Plus questions with Qwen 3.5-27B it reaches 82.6%, against 70.6% for MiroFlow and 62.5% for majority voting at equal token budget. Without the gathering gate, teams keep a wrong first candidate in 32.2% of runs instead of 22.3%. With GPT-5 on live-web BrowseComp it scores 73.6%, above GPT-5 alone at 54.9%. Orchestration design here is about controlling who sees what, and when.

On the data-agent side, Alon Halevy's group released [SynthCorp](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.14765), a platform that generates enterprise data estates by simulating how business events produce and modify corporate data over a virtual calendar. Each estate combines relational tables, a document store, and a data catalog kept mutually consistent, with gold programs derived from the simulation log. The first release has seven estates of up to 1K operational tables and 256 tasks across five domains. Two properties make this relevant to governed analytics: the benchmark separates performance across four data-context settings, so you can measure what catalog and documentation context buys an agent, and gold programs are verified by auditing candidate queries against causal provenance in the log rather than by hand annotation. Multi-event composite tasks still break state-of-the-art agents.

Watch whether anyone runs the tau2 boundary evaluator against production write logs, and whether SynthCorp's data-context settings get extended to a governed semantic layer as one of the conditions; that comparison against raw catalog context is the experiment the field still lacks.
