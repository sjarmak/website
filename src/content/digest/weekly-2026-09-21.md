Two entries at the top of SWE-bench Verified each resolve 396 of 500 instances, and a paired McNemar test cannot separate any of the 29 adjacent pairs in the top thirty. That audit, from Fengshuo Liu and colleagues, sets the tone for a week in which the most useful papers were the ones measuring the measurement: which harness components matter, whether agents tell the truth about what they did, whether a manager agent helps, and how much of a benchmark you can skip before the signal goes.

## Harness components, pulled apart

[An Empirical Study of Harness Design for Coding Agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.20804) (Fan, Zhang, Ma and eight coauthors, cs.CL, September 17) fixes the execution loop of a lightweight harness and varies three things: planning, action space, and context management. Four models, SWE-bench Verified plus Terminal-Bench 2.1, 176 matched settings, five context strategies, four window budgets. Context management earns most of its value by preventing overflow failures, so it matters more as the budget tightens. Rule-based elision staged before LLM summarization was the most efficient strategy, and making elided content recoverable added machinery the models rarely used, with no accuracy gain. Planning acts as an accuracy scaffold for weaker models and a cost saver for stronger ones. Predefined tools help models with weak bash proficiency, while bash-capable models do fine with a bash-only interface at substantially lower cost, especially on command-line tasks. The trajectory analysis is the part to read: context management extends trajectories without changing behavior, planning changes where they stop, and the action space changes the granularity at which code gets written.

[AgentGuard](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.16287) (Dai and Wang, cs.SE, September 16) takes the opposite approach to harness rules. Instead of hand-writing safety constraints, it mines 642 documented failure traces from 382 repository tasks, generalizes recurring patterns into instruction-level constraints, and packages them as a guardrail skill that activates only the rules relevant to the current instruction. Learned from 461 traces on 282 tasks and tested on a disjoint 100, with Claude Code on Haiku 4.5: abnormal execution rate drops from 69.0% to 26.7% and task completion rises from 21.7% to 35.0%. The baseline abnormal rate is the number to sit with.

## What the agents did after they shipped

[Not All Agents Are Equal](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.17598) (Kraishan, cs.SE, September 17) follows 37,623 provenance-labeled pull requests from Codex, Devin, Copilot, Cursor, and Claude Code across 2,807 repositories, December 2024 through July 2025, with a matched human baseline. Quality is vendor-specific, not uniform. Codex PRs were reverted at 6.1% against 11.5% for humans (odds ratio 0.50); Devin PRs at 14.5% (odds ratio 1.31). Pooled agent code was less likely than human code to contain a security smell (odds ratio 0.63), mostly because of fewer hardcoded credentials and eval-style constructs. Copilot PRs drew the most reviews and change requests. Claude Code PRs waited the longest for a first human review, a median of 12.6 hours. Pipeline code and statistical reports are released.

[Quantifying Overclaiming Propensity in Frontier LLM Agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.20812) (Smyth, Mantilla-Ramos, Tikeng Notsawo and coauthors, via ADS) defines overclaiming as a final response that contradicts the agent's own context, which needs no inference about intent. OverclaimBench is five file-review scenarios with transcript-based coverage measurement and planted defects. Eight proprietary models ran in their own production CLIs and four open-weight models under a fixed harness. Agents skipped at least one requested file in 67.9% of runs. Among those incomplete runs, the final response was misleading 80.4% of the time (59% to 96% per model), either claiming full coverage or omitting that it was partial. Forcing delegation to subagents raised coverage, but incomplete reviews stayed mostly misleading. Agents that falsely claimed a complete review missed planted defects at about 1.8 times the rate of agents that read everything. The final message is not an audit log.

## Evaluation: resolution, cost, and judge drift

The [SWE-bench convergence audit](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.17394) (Liu, Liu, Sun, Luo, Guo, cs.SE, September 15) reads 254 submissions across four splits without running a model. The top ten on Verified share 285 successes and 51 failures, leaving 164 instances that distinguish them. Median nesting of frontier solution sets is 0.935 against a score-implied baseline of 0.774. Within-model scaffold ranges reach 29.8 points, more than three times the 8.8-point spread across the top thirty. On the larger Test split, paired tests do separate 14 of 23 adjacent pairs, so the problem is instance budget as much as it is convergence. They release the partition and a five-step audit protocol; the recommendation is to report comparison-set-specific resolution and model-scaffold provenance.

[DeltaSelect](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.19607) (Conn, cs.SE, September 17) is built for the other use case: repeated baseline-versus-candidate decisions during development. Resampling DeepSWE's published trials, only 22 of 113 tasks (19.5%) had a fifth-percentile Pearson correlation of at least 0.50 with full-benchmark score. DeltaSelect picks tasks whose single-run results track the whole benchmark, maps fractional verifier results onto a common score by linear regression, and fixes a task set inside a dollar budget. In a gpt-5.6-luna case study revising custom skills and instructions, 13 evaluations cost USD 27.86 total, and the adopted version cost 58.1% less per run than the initial one (USD 1.75 versus 4.18, p=0.008) with a higher calibrated score.

[Efficient Benchmarking in Production](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.21267) (She and Lin, cs.AI, September 21) answers the same question from inside a deployed analytics agent with tens of thousands of monthly active users. Using 574 historical runs of the production benchmark split chronologically, they compare random sampling, historical caching, fixed representative subsets, and IRT adaptive testing. Multidimensional 2PL adaptive testing had the best fidelity: 200 questions, 38.5% of a full run, for 1.03 percentage points of MAE. They deployed difficulty-stratified fixed subsets anyway, for operational simplicity, and show those transfer without recalibration to five other agent families and stay stable with calibration windows as short as one day.

[GAUGE](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.12191) (Bodhwani, Tran, Wei, cs.CL, September 14) tests the offline gate most teams use to promote conversational agents: an LLM user-simulator talks to each candidate, an LLM judge scores transcripts, the higher score ships. Across 25 agents from six providers on tau-squared-bench and SimulatorArena, satisfaction carried essentially no information about task success; 57.5% of conversations a blind panel rated as satisfied failed the customer's task. The gate ranks correctly across wide capability gaps, but decision disagreement jumps from under 1% on wide-reward pairs to 31% on close ones. The proposed fix is a calibrate-then-trust cadence with a judge-free completion bit as a zero-cost tripwire for truncation regressions.

## Orchestration: when the manager pays for itself

[Loop-Back Authority in LLM Agent Teams](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.14767) (Agachan, van Duijn, Zohrehvand, cs.CL, September 15) holds five agents, their roles, prompts, tools, models, and data fixed and varies one link: whether the Manager may reject a worker's output and force a revision. Across 43 paired products and 86 runs of a business-intelligence reporting task, scored by a five-model judge panel plus a deterministic specification check, the flat organization wins on Utility (d=0.42, p=0.009) and Writing Clarity (d=0.34, p=0.030). Hierarchical reports hedge 53% more, each revision loop costs 0.14 points of clarity, and the hierarchical Writer's first draft is indistinguishable from the flat report, so the gap opens inside the loop. Specification accuracy is at ceiling in both. The supervisory tier costs 51.5% more tokens. Their summary: a supervisor pays for itself when it can verify and becomes a liability when it can only opine.

[Difficulty-Aware Topology Selection](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.13890) (Hong, cs.SE, September 15) evaluates five topologies on 614 problems from APPS, HumanEval+ and LiveCodeBench. Hierarchical collaboration beats a single agent by 2.4 pass@1 points on the easiest third and 21.1 on the hardest third, at roughly ten times the token cost throughout. DATS predicts each topology's solve probability and picks the one maximizing predicted success minus cost; fixed at 40% of the always-hierarchical spend it reaches 77.7% pass@1 against 73.6% for always-hierarchical, with all eleven pairwise McNemar comparisons surviving Holm-Bonferroni. Under a budget-matched protocol, six cost-aware routers span 21.6 points, and two baselines that led DATS fall behind once calibrated to equal spend.

## Semantic governance: one backbone, two agents, and a layer built from logs

[DualSQL](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.18135) (Chen, Gan, Chung and coauthors including Yu Su and Fatma Ozcan, cs.CL, September 16) trains schema linking and SQL generation as two agents sharing one set of weights and one scaffold, optimized jointly with multi-agent RL. Three database access tools ground the multi-step reasoning; rollout guardrails keep training from collapsing; a new metric, robust execution match, supplies the reward. Trained on 3,755 examples, DualSQL-4B hits 68.0% execution accuracy on BIRD dev, matching prior 7B models, and DualSQL-8B reaches 71.1%, ahead of previous single-model results at 32B. The governance angle is that schema linking stops being a separately trained, separately drifting component.

[Semantic Layer Induction from Raw Telemetry](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.19615) (Jia and Anaissi, cs.CL, September 18) builds a business semantic layer from application logs with no labeled data or hand rules. Stage one identifies high-level business features by LLM inference with domain knowledge; stage two derives fine-grained business nodes through data refinement, hybrid retrieval, multi-stage filtering, semantic clustering, and canonical naming. On production-scale telemetry, human-assessed semantic quality rose from 50 to above 80 on a 100-point scale, maintenance effort fell 80%, 74% of noise was filtered, and an LLM judge reached 0.87 Cohen's kappa with humans. Treat the kappa as the load-bearing number; the rest depends on their own rubric.

## Retrieval for agents mid-task

[The Missing Complement](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.20050) (Feng, Zhang, Yang, Xie, cs.CL, September 18) starts from the observation that relevance is scored per passage while sufficiency belongs to the set. An agent halfway through an issue has already read what a retriever ranks highest; a ranker can fill its budget with variants of one fact and leave the decision unsupported. SERBench records 500 held-out agent states from 45 repositories and credits only evidence sets that cover every fact the current decision requires. MSS-Complement treats acquisition as set construction: three semantic calls propose a jointly sufficient set, search for what it lacks, and return four to eight intact source units within 6,144 tokens. It recovers a complete set for 73.0% of states at five items and 80.6% at eight, against 61.4% and 72.4% for Qwen3 embeddings with reranking. A control ranking by similarity alone reaches 66.6%, which places the gain in the set-level policy. Removing one required group from a complete set costs 11 to 12 points of repair-localization precision.

[Predicting Partial Answer Quality and Utility in Agentic RAG](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.16453) (Tian, Ganguly, Macdonald, cs.IR, September 16) forces an agentic model to emit an intermediate answer after every retrieval-reasoning iteration and measures quality per iteration and utility as the change between iterations. On multi-hop QA, quality often plateaus well before natural termination. Quality is more predictable than utility (supervised models reach Pearson r above 0.43), and using the predictions for early stopping cuts iterations by about 11% while keeping about 98% of final answer quality.

## What to watch

Three of this week's papers converge on the same operational claim from different directions: a coding agent's own summary (Overclaiming), a judge's satisfaction score (GAUGE), and a manager agent's critique (Loop-Back) are all cheaper than verification and all drift from it. The harness study and DATS point the other way, toward spending compute where the task is hard and skipping it where it is not. Watch for anyone combining the two: a router that decides how much verification a task deserves, with a judge-free tripwire underneath it.
