In 11 of 2,160 tau2-bench episodes, an agent executed a database write the airline or retail policy forbade. An LLM judge reading the transcript passed 10 of those 11, and it kept passing them when handed the policy document. A stronger judge still passed 6. A rule-based check with no model call at all, one that compares whether a write was expected against whether one occurred, caught all 11. That result, from Sungbin Im's [When Evaluators of AI Agents Disagree](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.14581), sets the tone for this week: several papers measured the gap between what an agent appears to have done and what it did, and the gap keeps turning out to be large.

## The grader decides the result

Im's study scored every episode four ways: native gold-state scoring, an LLM judge, the same judge given the policy, and the minimal "boundary evaluator". The judge was consistent with itself, repeating its verdict 98.2% of the time, yet it agreed with native scoring on only 56.2% of episodes. Consistency is not validity, and a judge that is stable on the wrong answer will look reliable on any test-retest check. The paper also tried verification, correction, and safe-termination gates; forbidden writes ran at 11.1% of ungated episodes and the gates did not reduce them significantly. The traces suggest why: the gates' evidence digest dropped order status and customer authentication, so the gate rejected valid requests and missed the bad ones. Anyone building a summarizing guard in front of a write path should read that failure closely, because the summary is where the evidence went missing. Traces, prompts, and labels are released.

[Do Verifier Judgments Survive Abstraction?](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.18471) by Disha Sheshanarayana and Sai Linisha Reddy Goli generalizes the same problem. They freeze agent executions and show a verifier three views of each one: native, abstracted (composed tools, delegation boundaries, compacted histories), and restored. Across four benchmarks, seven verifier backbones, four protocols, and three abstraction boundaries, judgments flip in up to about 31% of cases under macro abstraction and delegation. Direct verification tends to produce wrong verdicts, while evidence-grounded and deliberative protocols tend to abstain, so a lower flip rate can just mean a verifier that declines to answer. Restoring execution provenance recovers 85.5% of the failures. Read together with Im's paper, the practical instruction is to keep the raw write log and the sub-agent trace attached to whatever you hand the grader; compaction and delegation strip exactly the evidence the grader needs.

## Coding agents finish early, stop late, and settle for less

[Termination Lag](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv261114960C) (Musa Cim, Burak Topcu, Mahmut Taylan Kandemir) recorded and tested every distinct patch a coding agent produced during a run, across five models, two frameworks, and three SWE-bench datasets. Between 38% and 82% of successful runs had a correct solution on their very first source edit. After that first correct patch, Qwen3-Coder spent a median 35 to 37% of its steps and Laguna-XS.2 spent 54 to 60%, mostly verifying and exploring. In 1 to 8% of cases the extra activity undid the correct patch. The cost argument is obvious; the more interesting implication is that a stop signal grounded in patch-level test results could save half the trajectory for some models without touching the solve rate.

The opposite failure got a name from Ruixin Wang and Tianyi Zhang: [specification satisficing](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.16289), where an agent considers an implementation that meets a requirement and then commits to one that doesn't, for a reason it finds acceptable. Self-correction methods inherit the bias because the agent already approved its own reasoning. SatisFix makes the agent list those choices and their justifications, then checks each one against the task description. On DeepSWE and the hard subset of SWE-bench Pro, across five backbones and two frameworks, it raised pass@1 by an average of 5.09% and 7.06% respectively, and beat the best self-correction baseline on DeepSWE by 3.98%.

Google engineers Sam Saccone and Madhuri Garikapati described a third derailment, [agent distraction](https://www.infoq.com/news/2026/11/google-agent-distraction/?utm_campaign=infoq_content&utm_source=infoq&utm_medium=feed&utm_term=global), as reported by InfoQ: stale TODO comments, obsolete bug references, and outdated specs in a codebase send agents on side-quests nobody asked for. Technical debt used to cost human reading time; for an agent that treats every comment as a possible instruction, it costs steps and correctness.

## Retrieval tools help small models by showing them the answer

Saurav Bellam's [pre-registered scale study](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.33958) is the most careful measurement of code-retrieval tooling I have seen. Nine open-weight models from Qwen3 0.6B to 32B and Gemma 4 E2B to 12B ran one localization harness on 500 held-out SWE-bench tasks under four tool sets plus a placebo arm whose structural tools returned random entities: 170,500 episodes with hypotheses frozen before the runs. Tools raised function-level Acc@10 by 0.06 to 0.10 for Qwen3 up to 14B, by 0.03 at 32B, and not at all for Gemma 4 12B. The embedding index carried the gain, not the code graph. The placebo confirms the effect comes from what the tools return, and random output actively hurt two small models. The mechanism is exposure: tools put the gold code in front of the model more often, and the 32B model already finds it unaided. Small models also act on a shown answer less often, with conversion rates of 0.09, 0.24, and 0.40 at 1.7B, 4B, and 32B. The pre-registered claim that a 4B model with tools matches a 32B model without them failed.

JioHotstar's internal comparison of six [code retrieval approaches for coding agents](https://www.infoq.com/news/2026/11/jiohotstar-codingagent-retrieval/?utm_campaign=infoq_content&utm_source=infoq&utm_medium=feed&utm_term=global), covered by InfoQ, landed in a compatible place from an industry angle: structural search did well on concrete tasks, semantic retrieval helped on ambiguous requirements, and the trade-off ran through accuracy, token spend, and tool design. Neither result supports "add a code graph" as a default; both argue for matching the retrieval tool to the task shape and the model's ability to use what it is shown.

## Multi-agent gains mostly vanish against a fair baseline

[More Agents, More Samples, or More Thinking?](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.17610) by Seyed Mahmoud Sajjadi Mohammadabadi pools nine empirical evaluations covering 516 multi-agent configurations and re-scores them against baselines matched on sample count or compute budget. Against those baselines, multi-agent systems average -0.042 in accuracy, and they equal or trail the baseline in 74% of comparable contrasts. Simply moving the comparison from a greedy single pass to a sampled baseline shifts the difference by -0.038 on average, which is most of the reported benefit. The argument is short: when agents share identical evidence, talking adds no information. Positive returns need partitioned evidence, external verification, or genuinely different errors across models. The paper ships a reviewer checklist; any team writing up a multi-agent result should run it before claiming coordination helped.

[WorkflowArena](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv261112856J) measures the thing that does vary: how a model organizes delegated work. Its 145 long-horizon tasks span eight domains from software engineering and formal math to legal analysis and 3D production, each with a hidden verifier, and every delegation is recorded. Across nine models no configuration wins everywhere. At high effort, Claude models in Claude Code launch workflows less often but with larger worker groups, while GPT models in Codex delegate more often in smaller units. Holding the executor fixed on hard tasks, workflows authored by Claude Opus 5.5 substantially improved weaker executors, so workflow design is a separable, measurable capability.

That same orchestrator-plus-cheap-worker shape has a safety cost. Keegan Wang and Anantika Mannby show in [Only as Safe as the Weakest Agent](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.17520) that when strong orchestrators search a file store with a planted forged record themselves, they commit almost no harmful revisions in over 1,000 trials. When they delegate the reading to a cheaper sub-agent, failures climb to 20/180, 44/180, 75/180, and 553/900 across setups, on unmodified Claude Code, Codex, and a reference harness. In simulation over 18 models, five aggregators that were almost never unsafe alone repeated a corrupted sub-agent's error in 751 of 757 trials. The system's safety sits near the weakest reader's.

## Semantic governance: grounding columns, tables, and time

Two Text-to-SQL papers this week treat grounding as a retrieval and disambiguation problem rather than a generation problem. [NIFT-SQL](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.12731) (Arjun Rao, Nishant Gopinath) trains per-customer models on each customer's example questions, reading only table names, column names, and declared keys, and fuses a fine-tuned retriever with a 560M cross-encoder that scores whether each table is needed. On BEAVER, LiveSQLBench, and LiveSQLBench-Large (244 to 971 tables), it puts every reference table in the top 15 for 69.2%, 89.5%, and 85.6% of questions, against 30.4%, 41.0%, and 21.8% for hybrid search. Customer-supervised schema linking is cheap labeling compared with a hand-built semantic model, and these recall numbers are the first stage any governed pipeline has to clear.

[Bridge](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.17770) (Sau Lai Yip, Yue Pang, Shiwen Wu, Xiaofang Zhou) attacks ambiguous column grounding. Offline, it builds a Column Ambiguity Graph of column pairs a question could plausibly confuse, verified by an LLM, plus a value similarity index. Online, it anchors on columns and values in a previous wrong SQL guess and pulls alternatives from both. On BIRD-dev without the benchmark's evidence hints, at comparable LLM cost, it beats the strongest clarification baseline by 11.60% execution accuracy and lifts column-usage precision by 12.37%. The Column Ambiguity Graph is a small, machine-built slice of a semantic layer: an explicit record of which definitions collide.

[BuySideFlow](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.12966) is the sharpest charter hit of the week because it measures time semantics directly. Its 408 practitioner-derived tasks run over a fixed snapshot of a licensed production financial database, and agents must respect disclosure cutoffs and pick the right historical record versions. Nine agent configurations score 16.2% to 36.8% on results. An expert audit of 900 runs found point-in-time violations in 193 of the 380 runs that passed the result check: 50.8% of "correct" answers used data the analyst could not have had on that date. That is the analytics version of Im's forbidden writes, a correct-looking output produced by an invalid execution.

Sohyeon Jeon's [NS-CoT for OMOP CDM cohort counts](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.10681) shows what a governed alternative looks like. An LLM interprets clinical-trial eligibility criteria into a structured intermediate representation with declared choices about concepts, temporal conditions, exclusions, and proxies; symbolic processing and deterministic SQL compilation produce the count, and every layer is logged. Compared with a direct-SQL baseline on synthetic OMOP data and a multi-site simulation, the gains vary by setting, and the author is explicit that the declared definitions are not clinical ground truth. The payoff is reviewability: layer-wise inspection located implementation and policy errors that could be corrected and re-run, which a single generated SQL string does not allow.

## What to watch

Three of this week's strongest results (tau2 forbidden writes, BuySideFlow's point-in-time audit, and verification transport) share a structure: the outcome check passes, and the execution record shows it should not have. Benchmarks that release write logs, record versions, and delegation traces alongside scores will be the ones worth trusting. The open question is whether harness vendors will expose that provenance to graders by default, or whether compaction and sub-agent summaries keep deleting it before anyone looks.
