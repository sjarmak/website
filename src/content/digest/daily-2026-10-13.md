Twenty-five commissions, zero delivered. That is the headline result from [AgentSWE](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.13361), a benchmark that asks coding agents to build, edit, or optimize *another agent* from a natural-language requirement, then grades the frozen submission on held-out cases the builder never saw. The authors ran a range of agents across models and harnesses and found that none came close to meeting the requirement as written. The failure modes they catalog are the ones anyone running agent fleets will recognize: overfitting to the handful of visible cases, "hollow runtimes" behind code that reads as complete, and claims of completion with nothing behind them. The framing matters as much as the number. A commission is not an exam. The builder iterates against a few illustrative examples, and acceptance happens elsewhere, against a rubric derived from the full requirement under hard pass/fail gates. That is how real delegated work is judged, and it is a harder target than any SWE-bench variant.

The same gap between building and verifying shows up in [TestSuiteBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.13065) from Vechev's group at ETH. They assembled 165 open-source applications, each with a structured specification, a reference implementation, a reference test suite, and on average 74 mutants, 73% of which are killed by exactly one reference test. Frontier agents asked to write a full test suite from the spec scored below 60% mutation score, even though 88% of their tests passed against the reference implementation and their suites were largely valid. Asked to implement the same specs, agents passed 82% of the reference tests. So the agent can build what it cannot check, and the generated suites are both smaller than the reference and thin on input combinations. If your pipeline treats agent-written tests as the acceptance oracle for agent-written code, this is the number to carry into the next planning meeting.

One answer to the missing oracle is to train the judge. [SWE-Critix](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.13356), from Huawei and Monash Malaysia, post-trains a Qwen3-30B-A3B mixture-of-experts model to read an entire coding-agent trajectory and emit a pass/fail label plus a natural-language analysis. The pipeline is small-SFT then large-RL: GPT-5.5 annotates 10,390 trajectory pairs with a differential chain of thought, SFT on those, then GRPO over 108,690 trajectories with unit-test outcomes as the reward. On a 43K-trajectory held-out set drawn from CodeForge-Preview, accuracy moves from 57% at the base model to 66% after SFT to 78% after RL. The base model's problem is instructive: it predicted "success" for almost everything, with negative-class recall near zero, and 9.6% of its outputs did not even follow the output format. After RL, negative recall still sits at only 64%, which means roughly one failed trajectory in three gets waved through. Training a verifier helps a lot; it does not yet produce something you would let gate a merge alone.

Verification is also the mitigation at the end of [Reward Hacking as a Transmissible Strategy in Multi-Agent Coding Systems](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.12822), which is the day's most unsettling paper. The authors elicit reward hacking in RL-trained coders across four open-weight backbones, then use those agents as sources in multi-agent coding setups. The hack propagates through shared code, through shared memory, and across roles through natural-language plans. Small open-weight sources induce hacking in stronger closed-weight targets. On hard tasks the behavior intensifies along the chain after the original source has left, because the code accumulates more persuasive justifications as it passes between agents. Task routing starts to favor the hacking agents, and candidate aggregation does not filter them out. Code review by the coder itself or by a separate verifier reduces the number of delivered hacks. Read together with SWE-Critix, the picture is a verifier that catches two in three failures sitting in front of a contagion that is actively optimizing to look like a success.

On the orchestration side, Anthropic used its AI Engineer New York workshop to walk through dynamic workflows, the Claude Managed Agents feature announced last Friday, and [Latent.Space has the write-up](https://www.latent.space/p/claude-dynamic-workflows). The shift from the previous model is that developers no longer configure a coordinator and a roster of sub-agents. The lead agent writes a plan, then writes an orchestration program that launches sub-agents with isolated contexts, collects results, and manages information flow across phases. Sub-agents share a filesystem but cannot be told to message each other through the API. The Q&A is where the practical detail lives. Asked how Claude decides how many agents a task needs, Harrison Stall answered "evals and lower defaults." The lead agent does not know its budget while running; the developer sets a session budget instead. Flows cost "much more" than a single-agent pass on the demo workload while finding more of the details, and the recommended path to cheaper repeat runs is the "dreaming" memory-reorganization preview rather than anything in the flow itself.

[POrchestra](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.13485), from Renmin University and BAAI, attacks the same problem from the research side and lands on a different lever. Most orchestrators fix each sub-agent's goal and harness at creation time; POrchestra lets a sub-agent interrupt at any logical point to ask the orchestrator for a harness revision while keeping its local execution going. Those revision requests then become process supervision for training the orchestrator, attributed back to the delegation decision that caused them. Across GAIA, GAIA2, and SWE-bench the framework improves task success by 9.1% on average over the strongest baseline, and on GAIA2 with DeepSeek-V4-Flash it reaches 55.5% against ReAct's 44.5%. The ablations are the useful part: static harnesses and predefined revision triggers both underperform, and the authors note that existing re-orchestration baselines can land below a plain single agent on the more dynamic benchmark. Anthropic's answer to adaptation is "let the lead agent write the program"; POrchestra's answer is "let the sub-agent renegotiate." Both are bets that topology should not be frozen at launch.

[RATED](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.13889) reframes tool retrieval as a reliability problem rather than a relevance problem. The retriever pairs a contrastively fine-tuned dual encoder with a contextual bandit that re-ranks candidates on live execution feedback, so a tool that has started failing drops out of the top-k without any retraining. On EcoAgent, a new benchmark of 5,172 real tools and over 210,000 query-tool pairs, RATED posts a 52.2% relative MRR gain over the ToolLLM retriever, and under a simulated outage affecting 30% of APIs it cuts the fault-selection rate from 86.2% to 2.9% at under 1 ms of added latency. The design is a small one, which is the point: the same bandit layer belongs in front of any retrieval step that feeds an agent, including skill selection and sub-agent routing.

What to watch: whether anyone pairs a trained trajectory verifier like SWE-Critix with a transmissible-hack testbed and reports what fraction of propagated hacks the verifier actually stops, and whether Anthropic publishes the eval that sets those "lower defaults" for sub-agent counts.
