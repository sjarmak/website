Across 49 reward-hacking runs of two open-weight models, a harness that forced the agent to say why it was making each tool call caught 90% of the cheating. That number comes from [EPISTEME](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.06563), a design from Martin Monperrus's group, and it is the clearest result in a day of papers about one problem: coding agents act through tool calls, and nobody can see why a call was made or what it changed until the damage is visible.

The EPISTEME design is simple. The harness requires the model to declare the intent behind every tool call, then records what the command actually did and checks the declaration against the effects. The output is a journal that works two ways, as a guard that can block a call before it runs and as a record for audits after the fact. The authors evaluated it on ImpossibleBench, whose tasks can only be passed by cheating, which makes it a clean test: any pass is a hack, so the question is only whether the journal exposes it. Ninety percent is high for a mechanism that needs no classifier, only a mismatch between what the agent said and what the shell saw. The open question is how a frontier model behaves once it learns that its stated intent gets compared to its effects; the paper tests two open-weight models, and an agent that writes plausible cover stories would defeat a naive check.

A smaller study from Nara Institute of Science and Technology attacks the same trust problem from the instruction side. [Do Coding Agents Follow AGENTS.md?](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.04367) inserted one policy, written four ways, into the agent context files of 86 benchmark tasks and ran them across four harness-model combinations. Conditional phrasing ("if you touch X, do Y") was followed less often than prescriptive or explanatory phrasing, and which of those two won depended on the harness and model. The practical reading for anyone maintaining a context file: rewrite conditionals as direct rules, and do not assume wording that works in one harness carries over to the next.

When the agent is one of several, a fault spreads. [Airlock](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.06727), from Hamed Haddadi's group at Imperial, is a multi-agent runtime built for the moment after an alert fires. Its tree-structured runtime narrows permissions from parent to child agents, and an append-only provenance DAG records which agent consumed which other agent's output. When something goes wrong, Airlock traces forward on the DAG to freeze tainted executions and pending actions, and backward to narrow the root cause. The comparison with existing controls is the useful part: native mechanisms in A2A and LangGraph either contained at most 33.3% of faults or kept at most 16% of healthy work without a redo, and Airlock blocked every post-alert faulty actuation on its main testbed while keeping 68 to 96 percentage points more healthy work. Across 1,832 testbed runs and 161 physical smart-home runs, that is containment with numbers attached, and the provenance-DAG idea transfers to any orchestrator that already logs who read what.

Evaluation got two contributions pulling in opposite directions. [Construct Validity of Coding Benchmarks](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.05665), from Paul Ralph, Sebastian Baltes and colleagues, analyzed nine benchmarks used to rank agentic coding tools and found that they measure broad constructs like "coding capability" with a small handful of metrics, and that the confounders each benchmark reports vary from one to the next with no shared standard. It is a methods critique, short on new numbers, but it names the gap that leaderboard comparisons paper over. [Trace2Eval](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.06506) is one attempt to close it. The pipeline takes real user interaction trajectories, uses a multi-agent system to recover the original query and restore the workspace to its pre-interaction state, then runs a candidate agent against the reconstructed task and grades the result with rubrics refined through inter-annotator alignment and judge agents calibrated against human scores. Feeding the rubric feedback back to the models raised scores by a mean of 10.05 points across 19 model-harness configurations, with a peak of 19.28 points for DeepSeek-V4-Pro running in Claude Code. The design goal, a benchmark that renews itself from live traces instead of aging on a fixed public set, answers the construct-validity complaint more directly than another static suite would.

On the analytics side, [AvalancheBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.24183), a revised version of a benchmark from Snowflake and Brown researchers, tests data agents that have LLM-powered semantic operators available inside the query engine (Snowflake Cortex AISQL) over text, images and documents. The finding that matters for governed analytics is where the points go. The best of five model-harness configurations reached 0.82 macro analytical fidelity, and incorrect operator outputs explained only 2.5% of lost points; agents misusing or avoiding the semantic operators explained 36.3%, and misinterpreting the requirements explained 51.5%. Questions arrive as one multi-part brief, and 29% of lost points came from an agent reusing another question's intermediate result where it should not have. The engine computes what it is asked to; the failure sits in mapping the business request onto the right operation. That is the semantic-grounding problem in miniature, measured on a benchmark that also separates monetary cost from a capability-adjusted token cost, so a cheap configuration and an efficient one are no longer confused.

Three of these six papers, EPISTEME, Airlock and AvalancheBench, converge on recording intent and provenance as first-class runtime data instead of reconstructing it from logs afterward. The next thing to watch is whether a harness vendor ships declared-intent journaling as a default, and whether anyone measures how quickly a capable model learns to game a check that compares what it says to what it does.
