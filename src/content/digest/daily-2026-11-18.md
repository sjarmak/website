Show an LLM verifier the same agent run twice, once as the raw trajectory and once after a delegation boundary or a compacted history has hidden some of the steps, and its verdict changes in up to about 31% of cases. That is the headline from [Do Verifier Judgments Survive Abstraction?](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.18471), one of five on-charter papers that arrived in arXiv's Monday 16 November listing, and it sets the tone for the rest: the number you get from an agent eval depends heavily on what the grader, the harness, or the SQL generator is allowed to see.

## Grader accuracy measured on one view of the run is not a reliability number

Sheshanarayana and Goli (submitted 13 November) freeze a set of agent executions and build three matched views of each one: native, abstracted, and restored. Task, trajectory, outcome, ground truth, criterion, and verifier stay fixed; only observability changes. Across four agent-evaluation benchmarks, seven verifier backbones, four verification protocols, and three abstraction boundaries (composed tools, delegation, compacted history), they call a changed verdict a "transport failure," and it peaks near 31% under macro abstraction and delegation. The failure modes differ by protocol in a way that matters for anyone choosing a judge setup. Direct verification tends to return a wrong verdict, while evidence-grounded and deliberative protocols tend to abstain when they cannot see enough, so a lower transport-failure rate can simply mean the judge declined to answer. Restoring execution provenance recovers 85.5% of the failures, and recovery drops when that provenance is lossy or missing. For multi-agent pipelines that grade a subagent's work from its summary, this paper measures the cost: if the evidence the judge needs sits on the far side of a delegation boundary, log it and ship it to the judge, or expect the score to move when your orchestration topology does.

## Similar solve rates hide very different agents

[Interpreting Coding Agents Through Their Behavioral Trajectories](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.17440) (submitted 12 November) takes the other side of the same problem, looking at what agents actually do rather than whether they pass. Badertdinov and Golubev parse 143,209 trajectories from 125 agent configurations covering 117 models into high-level actions (repository search, file edit, test run) with a deterministic rule-based parser, which they audit against a six-model consensus label on 500 events. Agents with similar solve rates explore, edit, and verify in measurably different ways, and the behavioral profile alone, built without task outcomes, token counts, or tool names, is enough to identify which agent stack produced a run across disjoint repository sets and languages. The authors flag that task composition is not fully controlled in the cross-repository case. They also show that scaffold choice and reasoning effort shift search, edit, and test behavior systematically, which makes the scaffold a first-class variable in any leaderboard comparison. The parser and an annotated subset are released, so a team running its own agent fleet can profile its own runs against the corpus.

## Models that rewrite their own harness mostly do not improve it

[Can LLMs Improve Their Own Harness?](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.30499), revised to v2 in the same listing, asks frontier models to build a complete harness for ARC-AGI-3 through a fixed coding bootstrap, play five development games through it, and then rewrite the whole codebase twice. Twenty held-out games, which never feed back into adaptation, score each version on Relative Human Action Efficiency (RHAE) alongside dollar cost. Across 27 lineages, harness quality ranges from zero to near-ceiling, but two revisions raise mean held-out RHAE by only 2.80 points, with a 95% interval of [-1.44, 7.35] that includes zero. Development-set gains overstate held-out gains, and cost reductions sometimes come out of task performance. The useful result is in the selection step: no revision in the study keeps its parent around, yet a post hoc rule that picks among existing snapshots by development score lifts the held-out gain to 4.53 points ([0.81, 8.68]). Generating a revision and deciding to deploy it are separate problems, and the second one is where a simple gate pays off. Anyone running self-modifying agent loops should keep the parent version and gate the replacement against held-out tasks, the same way a CI pipeline gates a merge.

## The aggregation rule is the attack surface in delegated work

[Securing Multi-Agent Systems by Design](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.17883) from Wang, Chen, and Gong targets the decompose-delegate-aggregate pattern most orchestrators use. Their response manipulation attack (ReMA) has a malicious subagent iteratively optimize its answer against a surrogate multi-agent system, in both untargeted and targeted variants, and existing aggregation rules fall to it. The defense, SecMAS, selects the response most semantically consistent with a sufficiently large subset of the other responses, and the authors prove security guarantees for both attack types and report it holding against adaptive ReMA variants at utility comparable to the rules it replaces. The abstract does not give attack success rates, so the size of the gap needs the full paper, but the design point is concrete: the aggregation rule in your orchestrator is a security boundary and should be chosen as one.

## Column ambiguity is a grounding problem, and the database can resolve it

The charter hit in this listing is [Bridge](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.17770) (Yip, Pang, Wu, and Zhou, submitted 13 November). The authors observe that many text-to-SQL ambiguity errors come down to which column a business term maps to, and that iterative clarification protocols resolve these with the LLM's parametric knowledge, which is expensive and often wrong. Bridge moves the knowledge offline into the database: it builds a Column Ambiguity Graph by flagging candidate ambiguous column pairs from database-grounded signals and verifying them with an LLM, plus a value-similarity index. At query time, the columns and values in a prior wrong SQL prediction serve as anchors to retrieve alternatives, which are pruned and handed to the ambiguity detector. On BIRD-dev with the benchmark's evidence hints removed, Bridge beats the strongest baseline by 11.60% in execution accuracy at comparable LLM cost, and raises column-usage recall and precision in the final SQL by 5.25% and 12.37%. The precision gain is the number to read closely, because wrong-column rate is exactly the failure a governed semantic layer is supposed to prevent, and here a precomputed, schema-derived artifact recovers some of it without a hand-built metrics layer.

The open question across all five is provenance. The verifier paper recovers most failures by restoring it, the trajectory paper builds a whole method on it, and Bridge is in effect provenance about which columns tend to get confused. Watch whether agent frameworks start treating execution provenance as a required output of every delegation and compaction step rather than a debug log.
