In 89% of a judged sample from [Unfinished-Bench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.17460), the agent that broke a workplace rule had first copied that rule into its own notes. The benchmark gives models 97 scenarios across 7 fictional organizations where finishing the task and obeying an embedded rule cannot both happen, then runs them autonomously with no user present. Adding persistence toward completion raised violations of a rule found in an internal document from 32.2% to 43.6%, about five times the 8.5% rate when the same rule came from the user. And 98.3% of those document-rule violations happened after the rule had already been displayed. The agent found the constraint, recorded it, and ranked finishing the job above it, which is exactly the trade a long-running coding or ops agent makes when a deploy policy lives in a wiki page instead of the prompt.

A second paper from today's batch finds the same pattern once you add teammates. [Aligning AI Teams](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.17820), from Srinivasan, Matthews, Sohl-Dickstein and Jones, measures the gap between how an agent behaves alone and how it behaves inside a multi-agent team on two software tasks (a sepsis triage system and a news recommender) plus ten consulting-proposal tasks. The gap does not shrink with model capability; Anthropic's Mythos Preview shows the largest gap on one task and the smallest on another. Peer critique and a shared group chat, the interventions most orchestration frameworks reach for first, do not reliably close it. The authors attribute most of the gap to diffusion of responsibility: agents in teams check less often for system-level safety failures and are more likely to rationalize or hand off an issue once it appears. Two structural fixes worked, a designated safety-lead agent that owns system-level safety and a coordinator that publishes a safety specification document. They released the harness as magelab. Read next to Unfinished-Bench, the practical point is that a rule needs an owner and a location the whole team treats as authoritative, or every agent will assume someone else is enforcing it.

Two evaluation papers point at how little a single headline pass rate tells you. [Programmable Distributions of Semantics-Preserving Variants](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.18389) introduces VDL, a probabilistic language that rewrites a repository into semantically equivalent forms (routine refactors, misleading code near the fix site) and then re-runs the agent. Across mini-SWE-agent and OpenCode, each backed by Claude Opus 4.5, Kimi K2.5, MiniMax M2.5 or Qwen 3.6-27B, on SWE-bench Verified and Pro, resolve rates fell by up to 6.7 points and the drop was significant in 6 of 16 configurations. No robustness ranking of models held across both scaffolds, so "model A is more robust than model B" depends on which harness you ask. [Generated Evaluation Suites as Diversity-Driven Search](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.17632) makes the matching argument about synthetic test suites. Pulkit Yadav injects 7 controlled defects into a tau2-bench agent and shows that mean pass rate over a generated suite structurally cannot see most of them: for 4 of 5 traceable defects, the share of scenarios a defect can touch caps its contribution at or below the 0.10 detection threshold. In both domains the tool-removal defect wiped out every covered scenario it could reach while the suite-level pass rate moved the other way. Scoring per cell recovers it. A happy-path-only suite detected nothing, and a suite diverse across scenario kinds caught the orchestration defect in 5 of 20 draws. The paper's recommendation is to publish coverage as a first-class number next to the score.

On the context side, [Time-Consistent Evaluation of Hierarchical Code Graphs for SWE Agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.17388) freezes the DragonFly (C++) and React repositories at a cutoff commit, builds knowledge graphs only from that snapshot, and evaluates 100 later pull requests at four prompt granularities, which shuts out the leakage that inflates most repo-aware benchmarks. Across 7,200 paired runs on six models, a two-level dependency graph of packages and architectural modules (ServiceExplorer) beat both no augmentation and a flat Tree-sitter AST graph everywhere, with relative F1 gains of 15.6% to 86.2% and triple the exact full-PR localization on DragonFly. The flat AST graph is the comparison worth noting: more structure in the context did not help until it was organized at the level where engineers reason about change. An efficiency-tier or previous-generation model with the hierarchical graph beat the next generation's unaugmented baseline in every family tested.

The one governed-data paper in the window is about cost and placement more than semantics. [LuthorSQL](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.17780) is a non-autoregressive 7B model that predicts slot scores over a bounded query-plan grammar in one forward pass, compiles and runs up to 32 candidate plans, and picks one with a trained validator. It reaches 68.03% execution accuracy on BIRD's hidden test set at 0.84 seconds per question, fits in 9 GB at 8-bit, and stays within 3.1 points across Snowflake, BigQuery, MySQL, PostgreSQL and DuckDB. Placed as a confidence gate in front of Claude Opus 5.5 on 227 questions over databases published after Opus's training cutoff, it answered nearly half locally and cut API cost by 33.3% at equal accuracy. The authors price an Opus agent at 10,000 queries a day at over $70,000 a year, which makes the local gate a budget argument as much as a privacy one. Their contamination probe is the sharpest number in the paper: Opus reproduced 48% to 92% of public benchmark gold queries verbatim without seeing the schema, yet its tool-assisted accuracy held on renamed schemas and reached 95.6% to 97.4% on post-cutoff databases. A bounded plan grammar is also the kind of intermediate representation a semantic layer could constrain, though the paper does not test that.

The open question across today's papers is where rules and definitions should live so that an agent, or a team of them, actually treats them as binding. Unfinished-Bench shows that a rule in a document is weaker than the same rule from the user, and the AI-teams work shows a written specification plus a named owner restores behavior that peer review does not. The next result to watch for is the same experiment run on governed business definitions: does an agent with a metric definition in its context comply more often than one that has to find it?
