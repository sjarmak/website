Maintainers of 20 GitHub repositories fixed 2,457 bugs over 18 months, and frontier coding agents pointed at those same repositories recover fewer than 21% of them. [PROBE](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.35987), announced today by Marco Paina, Mark Niklas Müller, Niels Mündler-Sasahara, and Martin Vechev, treats that fix history as a partial reference set and adds a matching procedure that decides whether an agent's finding corresponds to a bug a maintainer later repaired. Recall climbs from 8% to 17% as the underlying model gets stronger, while precision against the reference stays below 9% for every model. Low precision here does not mean the agents are hallucinating, because LLM and human evaluators judge 85% of the findings valid. The more useful result is the shape of what gets found: the distribution of bugs is similar across agents and differs substantially from the distribution maintainers actually fixed. A team that runs a second frontier agent as a cross-check is mostly buying the same coverage twice, and a team that scores its bug-hunting agent by asking whether each finding is real will report something like 85% and learn nothing about whether the agent catches the bugs that cost them releases. PROBE measures the second question, which is the one worth paying for.

[Continue or Restart?](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.35971) takes apart a cost-saving idea most agent platforms have considered: watch the first few thousand tokens of a run, kill the ones that look doomed, and spend the budget on a fresh attempt. Srinath Srinivasan, Lunxiao Li, Tim Menzies, and Wesley Assunção work from 147,002 execution traces across two public corpora and four model-framework settings, and they separate two things a monitor could be learning: how hard the issue is, and how this particular attempt at it is going. Early prefixes help rank outcomes across tasks. Within a task, where the stop-or-continue decision lives, discrimination sits at chance from 5k to 20k tokens, and a fine-tuned Qwen3-0.6B monitor evaluated over three seeds and 32,406 attempts lands at a mean within-task AUC of 0.50 [0.48, 0.51]. Learned restarting still beats matched random restarting by up to 1.98 points under a 75k-token cap, and an exact decomposition shows where that margin comes from: almost all of it is between-task allocation, meaning the policy learns not to stop on easy issues, while attempt-level selection contributes between -0.11 and +0.24 points. The comparison that matters most is against doing nothing. Uninterrupted continuation beats both restart policies on solve rate at lower mean token cost in all four settings at the 75k cap, and in 216 of 224 budget-threshold configurations overall. Anyone shipping an early-termination monitor now owes a within-task AUC and a net-compute comparison against simply letting the run finish.

The same gap between a reported signal and the thing it reports on shows up in multi-agent coordination. [PASS Is a Claim, Not an Observation](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.35950) re-verifies fix announcements from azone_god, a privileged coordination agent in AITRAP, a live multi-agent knowledge-tree platform. Of 179 coordination broadcasts, 28 carried structured fix assertions in mixed English and Chinese (PASS, checkmarks, "verification passed"), and Lei Zhenbing and Lei Chang checked 10 code-level claims three ways: whether file modification times fit the claim (70% passed), whether the claimed defensive pattern exists at the named identifier and call site (60% in the claimed file, 80% anywhere in the codebase), and whether a deterministic rule-based check could re-verify the claim from the broadcast alone (80%). Among claims with enough detail to re-verify, half failed the identifier-and-call-site check, so the fix was either somewhere else or absent. Ten claims from one platform is a case study, and the paper does not pretend otherwise. The design lesson carries anyway: an orchestrator that advances work on a subagent's self-reported PASS has left verification to the agent that wrote the fix, and the mechanical checks here, a timestamp and a search for the pattern at the named site, would have caught half the bad claims.

[HSC-Bench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.36523) measures the handoff itself, the point where one model passes unfinished work to another. Its 1,039 tasks split into two tracks: recovering the repository evidence the previous agent already found, and continuing from established conversational, tool, and file-work state without redoing completed work or breaking commitments already made. Across six receiving models, structured handoff state improves evidence recovery for every model and continuation for most, and the model that ranks first on evidence recovery is not the one that ranks first on continuation. That split matters for anyone routing follow-on work from an expensive planner to a cheaper model: a receiver that retrieves the right files can still ignore decisions the first agent committed to, and a memory-retention score will not tell you which kind of receiver you have.

Netflix's [Pensive AI write-up](https://netflixtechblog.com/making-failure-diagnosis-reliable-with-pensive-ai-6b0811a1c015), published Monday, is the production counterpart to these papers, and most of its reliability comes from what the model is not allowed to do. The data platform runs millions of workloads a day across Titus, Spark, Trino, and Druid, and roughly 100,000 fail on peak days. Legacy Pensive, regular expressions plus hand-written Java rules, made about half of failures self-service, but over one three-month window about half of its seven million classifications landed in "unknown." The AI extraction path numbers every log line and asks the model for two integers, the first and last line of the error span; Pensive reconstructs the text from its own copy of the log, so output cost stays flat regardless of stack-trace length and the displayed error cannot be invented, though the model can still pick the wrong range. Per-log calls share a deadline set inside Maestro's hard 90-second post-processing timeout, and calls that miss it are dropped from the response instead of delaying it. Evaluation uses the frozen legacy rules as a regression floor, two LLM judges for inclusion and precision, a dataset-wide script that flags lines appearing in more than about 30% of workflows (the resulting exclusion list is three lines long), and latency on every call. Because logs expire in 7 to 30 days, production requests archive their inputs to S3 without overwriting, and every pull request deploys to a preview environment where precision and inclusion thresholds gate the merge. AI extraction now covers 100% of failures. The team labels its 321% figure (more manual log exploration on failed attempts where nobody used Pensive AI) observational, and the next step it describes, letting the model classify restartability, comes with the cost asymmetry stated up front: marking a recoverable failure non-restartable turns it into a hard failure, an alert, and manual remediation.

The day's direct hit on semantic governance is [Grounding LLM Agents in Business Processes](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.37242) by Saiful Khan and Afrah Farea, which isolates how much of an enterprise agent's error rate comes from missing structure. Agents calling enterprise APIs have to pick the right endpoint, follow process order, use valid payload keys, and choose coded values from predefined lists, and retrieval that treats each endpoint as an independent document drops every constraint between them. The authors load OData metadata and BPMN reference processes into a typed property graph, retrieve candidate endpoints with dense embeddings, and then follow process edges to the endpoints that come next. On 42 enterprise services with process-chain ground truth, Recall@3 improves by 5.7 points on queries with low lexical overlap to the downstream entities (n=74), matches dense retrieval elsewhere, and gives nothing on OpenAPI services that lack a process layer, which is good evidence that the gain comes from process structure and not from the retriever. An agent on 81 multi-step queries completes 38.7% of tasks against 10.3% for a chunk-based baseline. The elicitation probe is the result to keep: supplying valid code lists eliminates invented codes but still leaves invalid keys in 46% of write runs, and only the full typed context brings invalid calls down to 3% (3 of 101 runs). Grounding one part of the schema contract fixes the error you grounded and leaves its neighbor in place, which argues for exposing the whole typed contract to the agent instead of whichever piece someone thought to retrieve.

On the retrieval side, [When Is Additional Retrieval Worthwhile?](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.37358) reports a result most RAG papers would bury. Zhouting Zhao trains a regularized linear router, frozen before held-out evaluation, to decide per question whether to upgrade from dense retrieval plus reranking to a hybrid lexical-dense retriever. On 60 unseen QASPER articles (188 questions, 169 with evaluable evidence), the router upgrades 24.5% of questions and matches always-hybrid evidence Hit@10 at 78.70%, against 76.92% for never upgrading. Paired paper-cluster bootstrap intervals do not establish a reliable advantage, and answers from a 4-bit Qwen3-4B show no clear gain in correctness or faithfulness under any of the three policies. The sample is small and the answer grading is model-assisted without human validation, and the paper states both. It still belongs beside PROBE and the restart study, since in all three a proxy metric moved and the outcome it stood in for did not.

What to watch: whether scoring against maintainer-fixed bugs replaces valid-finding rates as the way bug-hunting agents get graded, whether any vendor publishes a within-task AUC before shipping an early-stopping feature, and whether the 46% invalid-key rate under partial grounding reappears when someone runs the same probe against a governed semantic layer instead of OData metadata.
