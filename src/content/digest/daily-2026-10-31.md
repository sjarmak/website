About 88,000 of the 1.53 million English agent skills indexed by GitSkills contain at least one line telling the agent to proceed without approval, pass `--no-verify`, or keep working without checking in. That is roughly one skill in seventeen, measured in [When Skills Tell Agents Not to Ask: A Large-Scale Study of Oversight Directives in Agent Skills](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.37734) by Timofey Sanko and Yuan Tian, who split each SKILL.md into sentences, bullets, table cells and code lines, hand-labeled 2,097 units against a codebook of polarity, mechanism and scope, and trained a detector to cover the rest. Permission flags are the most common mechanism, and 70.1% of the loosening directives apply unconditionally rather than being scoped to a context like CI. The riskier the skill, the more likely it loosens: skills that run shell code, install packages or use sudo carry 1.7 to 1.8 times the odds of a loosening directive compared with skills holding the same amount of oversight language, and they add no tightening to compensate.

The spread is concentrated upstream, which makes it fixable. Half of all copies of loosening-carrying skills trace to 2.4% of skill families, and half of all loosening occurrences trace to 1.1% of distinct loosening lines, so adopters are copying the directive rather than writing it. Open-source skill scanners catch at most 46% of these directives at precision below 0.4; the same LLM those scanners wrap, prompted with the paper's codebook instead, catches 61% at 0.71. The model did not change between those runs, only the definition of what to look for, and reviewing the small set of upstream families that carry most copies would remove most of the exposure.

The other papers from the last day put the remaining checks around an agent under measurement, and most came up weaker than their users assume. [Soei: Structured Observability of Evidence and Intent for Multi-Agent AI Software Engineering](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.39468), by Mohammed Nayeem Khan and K S Sarun Kumar, instrumented 45 real coding-agent sessions (102,233 records, 20,577 tool executions, 2,336 human-to-agent episodes) and used a blind-audited classifier, with precision of 83.3% and 96.7% on its two labels, to ask whether edits were ever checked. An estimated 23.4% of code-editing episodes (95% CI 17.1 to 35.7) ended with no independent check after the last edit, and 57.8% of the to-dos an agent marked complete after editing had no proof between the edit and the checkmark. The sessions are long and leaky: 42% ran past 24 hours, 48.1% of transcript bytes sit beyond a 2 MiB replay bound, and 1,326 records across 16 sessions held credential-like strings. Before proposing anything, the authors audited the open-source Agenttrail tool against its source at commit e4ba2da, confirming nine documented mechanisms at file-and-line level while finding two event pipelines that never merge and an unauthenticated local mutation surface in its Map service. Their proposal is a hash-chained local ledger with a W3C PROV-aligned graph and a verification layer that records a completion claim and its proof as separate events; on the same corpus it stores 20% of raw size, holds no content or credentials, verifies about 126,000 events per second and replays deterministically. Forty-five sessions and two authors make the rates a baseline to reproduce, but any orchestrator that trusts a subagent's checklist should run the 57.8% test against its own logs.

The checks that run after a failure have their own blind spot. [Repairs for Whom? Evaluator-Executor Mismatch in Selecting Repairs for Failed LLM Agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.38610), from Yijun Liao and Fanwei Liang, holds failed trajectories and candidate single-step repairs fixed and labels each candidate with rollouts of the agent that will execute it. Against that ground truth, greedy replay gives up 0.08 to 0.13 in expected net success gain on ALFWorld and 0.06 to 0.13 in net reward on WebShop. Scoring repairs for Qwen2.5-7B with Qwen2.5-14B rollouts at an equal budget loses 0.25 on ALFWorld and 0.07 on WebShop, twenty 14B rollouts per candidate are worth no more than two of the 7B agent's own, and on cases where the two disagree, fresh rollouts confirm they prefer opposite repairs. Swapping between similar-sized models from different families costs 0.07 to 0.17 in both directions, and the scoring executor reorders which repair methods rank best. Any pipeline that sends review to a large model and execution to a cheap one is exposed here, since the larger model turns out to be a poor judge of what helps the smaller one. The authors' rule fits on a sticky note: verify a repair with the executor that will run it, and name that executor on the label.

The semantic-governance version of this problem comes from knowledge graphs. [What Does an Ontology Catch? Curated, LLM-Generated and Data-Derived Ontologies as Checkers of LLM Agents over Knowledge Graphs](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.37809), by Michal Orzechowski, Rund Tawfiq and Tomasz Kosciolek, studies the schema-derived checker instead of the agent. They compare a curated ontology, one an LLM writes, and one mined from the data, used as prompt context, as a checker, or both, across open-weight and proprietary agents on public graphs and a private graph of nearly 10^9 triples. A checker can miss errors, and it can flag correct answers, and the second failure is worse inside an agent loop because the agent then "fixes" a right answer. On UniProt the curated ontology flagged more correct answers than either the data-derived schema or one an LLM produced from a masked summary of the data, the curated schema as published sometimes contradicted its own data, and on real agent answers its flags were only marginally more precise than random flags. These are life-science Web graphs rather than a warehouse with a metrics layer, but the lesson carries straight over: a governed definition used to gate agent output needs its own false-alarm rate, because a stale or over-strict definition will push a correct agent into changing its answer.

Benchmarks are the checks on everything else, and their labels have a quality problem of their own. [Well-Specified and Well-Tested? Assessing SWE-bench-Style Datasets with a Self-Evolving Agent](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.39417), from Zhonghao Jiang and colleagues, builds ExLabel, an LLM annotator that keeps an evolving bank of past judgments and reuses relevant ones to rate whether an instance's issue is clear enough to solve and whether its tests cover a correct fix. To score it they cleaned the human annotations on SWE-bench and Multi-SWE-bench into SWE-TQ, a benchmark spanning eight languages. ExLabel posted the best F1 with all three backbone models, with relative gains over the strongest baseline of 44.2% and 49.9% on task clarity (Python and multilingual subsets) and 24.0% and 18.4% on test coverage. A coding model fine-tuned on SWE-rebench data filtered by ExLabel resolved 66.2% of SWE-bench Verified, 14.5% better than the same model trained on data filtered by that baseline, so the training-data filter belongs in any comparison of fine-tuned coding models.

Meta published the production numbers for a check designed to assume the code is wrong. [Catching Bugs Before They Land: JiTTesting's Human-on-the-Loop Approach at Full Scale](https://engineering.fb.com/2026/10/29/security/catching-bugs-before-they-land-jittestings-human-on-the-loop-approach-at-full-scale/) describes Catching JiTTests, which runs overnight on spare capacity against the previous day's diffs that a risk model flags. One workflow treats each diff as a mutant and generates tests that distinguish it from its parent; the other infers the diff's intent, writes out how that intent could be implemented wrong, builds a mutant for each risk and generates tests to kill them, keeping any test that fails on the real diff as a weak catch. An ensemble of LLM judges and a deterministic rule-based assessor called RubFake rank the weak catches, and engineers see only what clears the bar. Meta reports that 589 engineers, 64% of those contacted, engaged with the findings and rated 60% of them true positives, and that in the first half of 2026 the system caught 74 serious privacy bugs at diff stage while privacy SEVs fell 20%. Those are engineer-reported precision and a before-and-after count, and the post does not separate the system's effect from anything else that changed in those six months. The pairing is what to borrow: LLM judges supply semantics, RubFake supplies verdicts that are deterministic and auditable, and neither is trusted alone.

On retrieval, [Contextual Embeddings Beyond the Gold Chunk](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.38854), from Sedigheh Eslami and coauthors, drops the assumption that a relevant document holds exactly one relevant chunk. Their pplx-embed-v2-context models learn graded chunk-level relevance distilled from a query-aware context compression model, which removes the need for gold-chunk labels and makes the training target independent of chunking; they ship in 9B and 0.6B sizes with compatible embedding spaces on a vision-language backbone. The two new benchmarks are the contribution I would reuse, a controlled Context-bench and Q2D-Chunk built from real search queries and released as a public leaderboard, because both label the chunks that answer a query and the chunks needed to verify that answer. The 9B model reports state of the art on both and on ConTEB, plus the best dense-retriever score on the visual ViDoRe-V3, with gains from finer image chunking. A retriever feeding an agent that must cite its evidence has usually been graded on the answer chunk alone, and Q2D-Chunk gives it a public leaderboard that grades the verification chunks too.

The numbers these papers had to dig out by hand are the ones that should ship by default: loosening directives per skill family in a registry, completion events with no proof behind them in an orchestrator, correct answers rejected by a semantic layer's validation, and the executor named on every repair label. None of them is a standard metric yet. Watch for the first skill registry or agent harness that publishes one, and for whether anyone changes a skill because of it.
