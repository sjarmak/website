A meta-analysis posted to arXiv yesterday pooled 516 multi-agent LLM configurations from nine studies, and against baselines matched on sample count or compute budget, the multi-agent systems came out at an average of -0.042 accuracy. In 74% of the fair comparisons, adding agents that talk to each other tied or lost to simply sampling more from one model. That number frames most of what came through the feed in the last day: the gains people attribute to architecture keep turning out to be compute, sampling, or a better grader, and the work that holds up is the work that changes what the agent can see or check.

## Multi-agent gains mostly disappear under a fair baseline

[More Agents, More Samples, or More Thinking?](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.17610) is a single-author synthesis, and its main contribution is accounting rather than a new system. It sorts baselines into a ladder: a single greedy pass, a baseline matched on sample count, a baseline matched on total resource budget, and so on. Most published multi-agent results compare against the bottom rung. Move up to a sampled baseline and the accuracy difference shifts by -0.038 on average across the studies that report both; random-effects modeling on the log-odds scale stays negative under every sensitivity check the author ran. The mechanism argument is the useful part: when every agent reads identical evidence, the conversation adds no information, so positive returns need partitioned evidence, external verification, or genuine error diversity across different models. The paper ships a reviewer checklist and accounting utilities, which is the right artifact; anyone evaluating an orchestration framework should be asking which rung its headline number was measured against.

## Training on the last mile transfers

The counterpoint comes from the training side. [Cross-Benchmark Transfer from RL on Agentic Coding Tasks](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.00890), revised this week, post-trains Kimi K2.7 Code (1T parameters, 32B active) with RL alone on 1,700 expert-built tasks: 1,000 repository tasks graded by hidden fail-to-pass and pass-to-pass tests, and 700 terminal tasks with expert-written hidden verifiers. The reward design is the interesting choice, since it is the fraction of target checks passed and drops to zero if any pass-to-pass test fails, so breaking existing behavior wipes out the episode. One epoch of GSPO on a rank-32 LoRA adapter lifts every external benchmark they tried: SWE-Bench Pro from 60.1 to 64.8, DeepSWE from 31.0 to 43.4, Terminal-Bench 2.1 from 67.4 to 82.0, and SWE-Marathon from 5.0 to 25.0. The gain holds on three task sets released after training data was collected (p = 0.004) and under two harnesses never seen in training, and median trajectories got 24-35% shorter. The authors frame the target as four last-mile failures: dropping a requirement, testing only the cases the implementation already handles, breaking preserved behavior, and validating against an unchecked assumption. Paired trajectories show the trained model avoiding each one on newly solved tasks.

## Lookups decide whether an agent writes to the wrong account

[What LLM Agents See and What Users Say](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.17639) is the most practically pointed paper of the day. It uses presupposition theory from linguistics to ask what stops an agent from cancelling the wrong reservation: an act is safe only if each claim behind it is observed, confirmed by the user, or disclosed while the act can still be undone. Across 36 earlier agents under a role-only prompt, a search that reports only a *count* of a second matching record leaves most of them acting on the one record they can see; a search that *lists* both stops most of them. The newest Claude and GPT models behave differently, always opening the counted second record and then declining to act. The retail-benchmark experiment is the one to show your team: add a same-name, same-zip customer and a lookup that returns that customer first produces wrong-account writes in about a quarter of conversations, while a lookup that checks a street address the user knows keeps completion near the single-customer level. The fix lives in tool design, and the evaluation lesson is that benchmarks need to vary what tools return, since a clean one-match fixture never exercises the failure.

## Review and grading as the control surface

InfoQ's [writeup of how the Claude Code team reviews code](https://www.infoq.com/news/2026/11/claude-code-review/?utm_campaign=infoq_content&utm_source=infoq&utm_medium=feed&utm_term=global) describes a layered setup for a codebase where agents contribute a growing share of changes: automated verification first, AI review second, and human approval routed by risk, with the stated goal of cutting manual review time without raising regressions or incidents. The writeup is thin on numbers, but the shape matches the RL paper's reward: cheap mechanical checks on preserved behavior gate everything, and human attention goes where the blast radius is.

[GDP.xlsx](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.17766) shows how far frontier models remain from passing that kind of gate on enterprise data work. It pairs 70 workplace requests from professionals in 13 domains with the full workbooks they refer to (2.18 million populated cells) and 791 binary rubric criteria, including completeness and exclusivity checks that require set-valued answers with nothing missing and nothing extra. An agentic judge opens the file to grade. Scored on strict pass@1, the best of 20 configurations, Gemini 4 Argon, passes 38.3% of attempts and the median is 19.3%. Only three tasks are public; the other 67 are held out against contamination.

## What idle subagents cost

From the practitioner side, a [r/ClaudeCode post](https://www.reddit.com/r/ClaudeCode/comments/1xx5405/broken_prompt_caches_cost_me_106_across_57_claude/) from the author of the open-source coding.sh tool reports on 57 of their own multi-agent workflow runs (666 subagents, about $2,500 API-equivalent). Broken prompt caches added $106, roughly 4% of spend, and 41 of those breaks came from subagents idling at a barrier until their cache expired while waiting on siblings. A paired A/B of a popular "be concise" plugin (10 tasks, 6 runs each) cut reply length 17% and output tokens 8% but raised cost per run 11% (95% interval +7% to +15%), because the style guide rides along on every request and most of what a coding agent emits is tool calls and code. Mining 3,600 sessions also surfaced a find-and-replace typo in an AGENTS.md that sent Codex hunting for a nonexistent folder in 92 sessions. These are one person's numbers, but barrier-induced cache expiry is a cost that orchestration frameworks rarely surface.

Put the meta-analysis next to the cache post and a question falls out: if fan-out rarely beats matched-compute sampling on accuracy, and synchronization barriers impose a measurable cost on top, which multi-agent designs justify themselves once someone runs the Category C baseline and counts the idle time? The presupposition paper suggests where to look for wins that survive, in agents that see partitioned or conflicting evidence that a single pass would miss.
