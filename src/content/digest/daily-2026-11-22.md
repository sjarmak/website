A production conversational-analytics system attached a hand-tuned confidence score to every text-to-SQL answer it wrote, and used it to show, warn on, or withhold each one. An audit of that score on the 1,534 questions of BIRD dev ([arXiv 2610.17256](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.17256), revised November 2) found it separated right from wrong answers at an AUROC of 0.624 and withheld 35.7% of the answers that were correct. Almost all of the signal came from agreement among five GPT-5.1 samples; most of it was destroyed when that agreement was combined, by a minimum, with a checklist that dropped to zero on any unsupported claim, commentary included. The authors then froze and hashed a simpler rule before any Spider answer existed (agreement with the shown answer is the score, plus deterministic vetoes and caps) and AUROC on Spider dev rose from 0.566 to 0.725, a gain of 0.159 with a 95% interval of 0.097 to 0.221. Two later findings matter more for anyone running governed analytics: a GPT-5.1 judge asked for P(correct) ranked higher still (0.837 on Spider), but it grades its own model's answers and its edge shrinks under execution-match labels, while adding one answer from a second model family raised agreement's AUROC from 0.749 to 0.800 under both label definitions. The paper notes that its labels are model-judged and a full human audit has not been done. The useful lesson is that a confidence gate is a measurement instrument, and its thresholds need the same validation as the model they sit in front of.

A second semantic-governance paper looks at the step before any query runs. [Can LLM Agents Be Forward-Deployed Engineers?](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv261103551M) (arXiv 2611.03551) puts agents into a real enterprise text-to-SQL engagement: a simulated customer opens with an ambiguous request, and the agent must discover acceptance criteria, data permissions, and hardware constraints, then plan and run a post-training recipe. Across 15 models the strongest discover over 80% of withheld requirements and open-weight models find 40 to 50%, but in delivery only 25% of runs across eight coding-agent configurations pass every acceptance criterion. Forty percent of withheld requirements end up missing or misrepresented, and the authors name benchmark accuracy not implying production readiness as one of four bottlenecks. Data permissions sitting inside "requirements the agent has to go find" is the uncomfortable detail; in a governed deployment those should arrive as a specification, never as something an agent may or may not elicit.

The evals story with the largest blast radius is [Benchmarks as Software](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv261108417B) (arXiv 2611.08417), from the Terminal-Bench team. They ran a continuous-validation workflow (community reports, agent traces, automated oracle checks, human-gated repairs) over Terminal-Bench 2.0, a benchmark that had been red-teamed before release, and found issues in roughly 30% of it. The repairs ship as Terminal-Bench 2.1: accuracy rises by more than 10 points and leaderboard positions move by up to three ranks, though overall ranking stays correlated at Spearman 0.958. The finding I would put in front of anyone comparing harnesses is about time: extending the wall-clock limit closes a 20-point harness gap on GPT 5.4 and shrinks a 30-point gap on GPT 5.4 mini to 5 points. A harness comparison at a fixed timeout is partly a comparison of how fast each harness spends its budget, and the paper argues, correctly, that resource settings belong in the measurement specification.

On orchestration, [Agent Architecture Cost-Success Trade-offs](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.12953) (November 11) holds model, tasks, and tools fixed and varies only architecture across 13 enterprise-style SOPs: a function-calling single agent, a skill-augmented agent, centralized multi-agent orchestration, and a triage swarm, in eight arms. No architecture is statistically separable in aggregate. Every coordinated arm spends 1.4 to 4.7 times the tokens and 2.6 to 5.8 times the latency of the single agent for gains that are usually small and rarely significant. High-quality skills lift success from 67.4% to 73.1%, degraded skills fall below baseline, and the only arm to beat the single agent significantly (75.6%) is a targeted redesign of a multi-agent implementation that had first lost to it. Implementation quality, in other words, decides more than topology does.

When you do split work across agents, [Signed but Not Safe](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv261107483C) (arXiv 2611.07483) shows where requirements leak. In a preregistered study the orchestrator extracts each requirement as a verbatim quote plus a checkable predicate, code signs the manifest with HMAC-SHA256, and deterministic code verifies the merger's decision. With Qwen3-8B over 1,440 trajectories, signed manifests cut unsafe commits under attack from 69.4% to 9.0%; the deterministic verifier caught 78.7% of violating decisions against 59.0% for an LLM checker, with 7% false alarms against 50%. Then the boundary: one adversarial line injected before signing pushed violations from 8.3% to 75.0% on GPT-5.6-luna, and the signature faithfully authenticated the corrupted manifest. Signing protects requirements in transit; extraction is the trust boundary, which is the same place the forward-deployed-engineer paper found its 40% loss.

For coding agents specifically, [Executable but Unfaithful](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.14629) (revised November 6) builds MockBench, 165 tasks for generating mock objects, stubs, and mock assertions in real Java and Python projects, and evaluates six LLMs and three coding agents. Agents raise executability well above plain LLMs, but that gain does not reliably carry over to behavior fidelity or to detecting interaction faults, and the authors' taxonomy of 660 failures documents deviations that a passing test run hides. A mock that compiles and passes tells you the agent satisfied the harness, which is a weaker claim than that it modelled the dependency.

Three of these papers land on the same measurement: the gap between what a check accepts and what was actually required, whether the check is a confidence gate, a benchmark verifier, or a green test suite. Watch whether Terminal-Bench 2.1's practice of publishing time limits and repair logs spreads to other agent leaderboards, and whether any governed-analytics vendor publishes the AUROC of its own answer-withholding score.
