# I put Temporal around my software factory. Here's what it fixed, and what it didn't.

*What I learned adding durable execution to a fleet of coding agents built on Gas City*

## Cabo and Beads

In the fall of 2025, I sat next to Steve Yegge at dinner during a Sourcegraph offsite in Cabo.

Steve had brought his laptop to the table, because of course he had (I was just running mine on my phone instead so I couldn't judge). Terminals running agents were scattered across his screen in a rainbow of different colors, and he kept moving between them while everyone else was doing more normal dinner activities.

I asked what he was building, and I remember the answer being something like, "Something that's going to be big."

A few weeks later, in October 2025, he published [Introducing Beads](https://steve-yegge.medium.com/introducing-beads-a-coding-agent-memory-system-637d7d92514a), a task and dependency system designed around how coding agents actually work. So the thing on the laptop at dinner really did become big. It was [Beads](https://github.com/gastownhall/beads).

<OpeningFigure />

Every new coding-agent session starts with some degree of amnesia. The previous agent may have investigated the codebase, ruled out two approaches, found a broken test, and created a reasonable plan. Then its context fills up or the session ends, and the next agent wakes up knowing none of it. You can try to solve this with Markdown files, but then the agent has to keep the plan current, understand which tasks depend on which other tasks, and avoid creating a whole ecosystem of slightly different planning documents (v1_final_FINAL_this-one-for-real.md).

Beads gives agents a dependency-aware work record instead. An agent can record what it discovered, connect related work, mark blockers, ask which tasks are ready, and return in another session without reconstructing the entire project from a transcript. I adopted it quickly. Then Steve built something much larger on top of it.

On January 1, 2026, he published [Welcome to Gas Town](https://steve-yegge.medium.com/welcome-to-gas-town-4f25ee16dd04): an opinionated coding-agent factory built on Beads, with persistent agent identities, disposable sessions, reusable workflows called formulas, coordination through mail and nudges, and a Mayor agent, the top-level coordinating agent and human handoff point.

It also came with Polecats, a Deacon, a Witness, a Refinery, Rigs, Convoys, Molecules, Wisps, and enough Mad Max terminology that I understood each concept slightly less after learning the name of the next one.

I forked it in early January with lofty goals of making it more vendor-agnostic, and chatted with Steve about that (to which he responded "send PRs!"). But I never quite got my first town working the way I wanted, and I had a critical project at the time ([CodeScaleBench](https://github.com/sourcegraph/CodeScaleBench)) that couldn't afford to stall on dropped queues and other random orchestrator nonsense. I reluctantly abandoned my ghost town. I could run a bunch of coding agents, but I didn't know how to keep a town of them from catching fire (or, more typically, have everyone sitting around doing nothing with half-baked work poorly translated like a horrible game of Agent Telephone).

The recovery idea underneath Gas Town is what Steve calls **Nondeterministic Idempotence**, or NDI: the exact path through the work does not need to be reproducible, because a fresh agent can inspect the durable work record and choose another valid route to the outcome.

In March 2026, before its public launch, I found the project that became [Gas City](https://github.com/gastownhall/gascity). Gas City, created by Julian Knutsen and Chris Sells, took the primitives behind Gas Town and turned them into a composable orchestration SDK. Gas Town was one specific city design. Gas City gave you the building materials to create your own. That separation immediately made sense to me, and I began contributing heavily in early April, eventually handling [over two hundred pull requests](https://github.com/gastownhall/gascity/pulls?q=is%3Apr+author%3Asjarmak+is%3Amerged) across session lifecycle, reconciliation, Beads integration, formulas, dispatch, the API, repair controls, and external messaging. Julian invited me and a fellow avid contributor to become Gas City's first outside maintainers.

I now use Gas City to run work across my many, probably too many, projects, including Gas City itself. The city builds and repairs the software that operates the city, which makes it exceptionally good at exposing its own failure modes.

One of those failures became the reason I added Temporal.

## The task survived. The procedure did not.

A coding agent could be midway through work when the orchestration process coordinating it died. Beads still retained the task and its state. What disappeared was the procedure that had claimed the task, launched the agent, and was waiting to record the handoff.

A work item becomes ready. The coordinating agent claims it and launches a coding agent. The agent gets a worktree, edits files, runs tests, and works for an hour. Then the coordinator's session dies before it records the handoff.

What should happen next? The system could launch another agent and accidentally leave two processes editing the same worktree. It could wait forever for a process it no longer knows how to contact. It could release the claim and ask a repair loop to infer what happened from whatever evidence the first process left behind. At various points, I have operated versions of all three.

The work record usually survived. The facts about the task were still there. But the procedure existed as a collection of clues spread across controller ticks, session records, mail, hooks, process state, and repair logic. At enough scale, reconstructing the procedure from those clues becomes expensive and ambiguous. I saw stranded claims, duplicate sessions after retries, stale completions arriving after a newer generation had started, lost notifications, and correlation metadata pointing at executions that did not exist.

The problem was not that Beads had failed to preserve the work. It had done that part. The problem was that my many agents and I had hand-rolled a distributed procedure without giving that procedure one durable owner.

> A durable work record is not the same thing as a durable procedure.

The rest of this article is what it took to act on that sentence. Here is where acting on it stands today:

| Current status | What that means today |
|---|---|
| Production | Durable result delivery and exact acknowledgement run continuously in my live installation. |
| Shadow | Temporal-controlled work claiming and agent launch is built and tested, but blocked from mutating production work. |
| Proven, bounded | One live bounded canary ran one claim, one agent session, one fenced receipt. A recorded single-host harness run proves Worker-kill recovery without a second agent. |
| Not claimed | Unrestricted production rollout of Temporal-driven agent execution, and cross-host recovery. |

## Two kinds of recovery

It would be easy to frame this as "nondeterminism is bad, so make everything deterministic," but that's not the solution. We need nondeterminism: it is the agent's individual actions that culminate in produced work. The question is where to draw the line.

NDI is one answer, and it is a good one where any reasonable path to an acceptable outcome is valid, which describes a lot of agent work. An agent can inspect a bug, choose a repair, run tests, realize it was wrong, backtrack, and try something else. I do not need a workflow engine to replay every thought or tool call in exactly the same order. I actively do not want that. Coding itself is nondeterministic: two agents can solve the same issue in very different ways, and either result may be acceptable (unless you're a [SWE-Bench verifier](https://openai.com/index/why-we-no-longer-evaluate-swe-bench-verified/)).

But some parts of an orchestration system are promises rather than work:

* Did this claim already start an agent?
* Does this result belong to the current generation?
* Has this exact outcome been acknowledged?
* Is a retry resuming the same execution or creating a competing one?
* Which recovery action owns the next transition?
* Should a late completion still be allowed to mutate the work record?

Those are procedural questions. "A capable agent will inspect the clues and figure something out" is not always the guarantee I want around them. The flaw was asking an NDI-style recovery model to own guarantees whose ordering, retries, waits, identities, and acknowledgements were themselves part of the contract.

By this point, my research on how coding agents fail had already convinced me that reliability depends as much on the system around the model as on the model itself, so I went looking for systems that had solved adjacent problems.

Because Gas Town is often described as "Kubernetes for agents," I started with Kubernetes controllers, reconciliation loops, leases, and ownership. Kubernetes can determine that a process disappeared and start another one. It does not inherently know whether a particular work claim already launched this particular coding-agent session, or whether a human acknowledged the exact outcome the system is waiting for. The broader distributed-systems toolbox gave me many necessary pieces (transactional outboxes, stable identities, idempotency keys, generation fencing, explicit acknowledgements, leases and heartbeats, retry-safe adapters), but no durable owner for the sequence connecting them.

Then I remembered Steve saying Gas Town looked like what you'd get if Kubernetes and [Temporal](https://docs.temporal.io/temporal) had a very ugly baby together. He had described NDI as similar to Temporal's deterministic durable replay, implemented through completely different machinery, and he was explicit that Gas Town was not a replacement for Temporal.

Temporal's model works like this. A [Workflow](https://docs.temporal.io/workflows) records a deterministic procedure in an [Event History](https://docs.temporal.io/encyclopedia/event-history). When a [Worker](https://docs.temporal.io/workers), the process executing Workflow code, crashes, another Worker can replay that history, reconstruct the Workflow's state, and continue from the recorded boundary. External and nondeterministic effects run in [Activities](https://docs.temporal.io/activities).

That gave me two recovery models to hold side by side:

| | Coding work | Procedural promises |
|---|---|---|
| Character | Nondeterministic | Deterministic |
| After a crash | A new agent may find another valid route | Ordering and acknowledgement must remain exact |
| Recovery | Inspect the durable work record | Replay the durable history |
| Owner | Beads and Gas City's NDI | A Temporal Workflow |

The question that became the project: could the setup I run with Gas City use both models at once?

## The boundary I chose

I did not want to put the coding agent's reasoning inside deterministic replay. That would be both impractical and just plain wrong. I wanted to keep the agent nondeterministic while making selected promises around it durable. The boundary I arrived at:

> Put the unpredictable agent inside an Activity. Put the promises around it in a Workflow.

The integration settled on three owners.

**Beads owns work state**: tasks, dependencies, claims, generations, artifacts, outcomes, and acknowledgement receipts. In my setup the Beads store (running on a [Dolt](https://github.com/dolthub/dolt) server) remains the canonical record for mutable work facts. Temporal does not replace it; a user or agent can still inspect the work independently of Temporal.

**Temporal owns procedure**: which orchestration steps occurred, which retry is active, what the system is waiting for, and what may safely happen next. Waits, retries, cancellation, signals, and acknowledgement progress.

**Activities own effects**: agent processes, tests, reviews, database calls, and external messages. The coding agent remains unpredictable, and Temporal records that the Activity was scheduled, tracks its heartbeats, and decides what to do if the Worker disappears.

<ConceptFigure kind="ownership" />

Stable identities, idempotency keys, and generation fences are not Temporal features. They are application responsibilities, and Temporal's guarantees are only useful because of them.

## The three failures I wanted Temporal to stop

The implementation focused on three concrete breakpoints. The status box above says where each one runs today.

### 1. A crash between claiming work and launching the agent

**The failure.** The system could record that work was ready and then die while trying to deliver the event that launched its execution.

**What Temporal contributes.** The ready transition and its delivery event are written together in one Beads transaction. A bridge reads the pending event and uses Temporal's [Signal-With-Start](https://docs.temporal.io/develop/go/workflows/message-passing#signal-with-start) operation with a stable [Workflow ID](https://docs.temporal.io/workflow-execution/workflowid-runid) derived from the city, run, and work item. If a Workflow with that ID is already running, the event is signaled to it. Otherwise, Temporal starts it and delivers the signal as part of the same operation.

**What stays the application's job.** There is still an unavoidable crash window: Temporal can accept the event, then the bridge can die before acknowledging the outbox record. The fix is not pretending that window disappeared. The event is delivered again, and the Workflow ignores an event ID it has already processed. Stable identity turns redelivery from a dangerous anomaly into normal, expected behavior. That guarantee is bounded by how long Temporal retains closed executions: past the retention window the ID can be reused, which is why the generation fence and the canonical work identity, not the Workflow ID, are what keep a late duplicate out.

**The evidence.** A test against a real Temporal dev server delivers the same ready event twice and requires exactly one scheduled Activity. The bounded canary exercised the path end to end.

### 2. A retry accidentally starts a second agent

**The failure.** Temporal Activities may execute more than once. A Worker can die after an external effect succeeds but before Temporal records its completion. That means "Temporal will retry it" is not, by itself, safe for starting coding agents: a naive retry could launch a second agent against the same work.

**What Temporal contributes.** Retry scheduling, [heartbeats](https://docs.temporal.io/develop/go/activities/timeouts#activity-heartbeats), and cancellation delivery, all recorded durably.

**What stays the application's job.** The Activity claims one exact work generation and receives a fencing token. A trusted adapter uses that token to resolve the agent session: it either starts the session once or returns the session already bound to that claim. What keeps a retry from becoming a second agent is not the heartbeat. A Worker can die before the first heartbeat ever lands. The retry presents the same claim token, and the resolver finds the session already bound to that claim by its stable identity, so there is no competitor to launch. The persisted heartbeat details are how the new attempt picks up progress from the last checkpoint, not how it avoids starting a second agent. Completion also goes through the fence: Beads accepts a receipt only when the work ID, generation, claim token, and the claiming Workflow identity still match. A stale agent can complete successfully and still be rejected, because it no longer owns the right to mutate the current work generation.

**The evidence.** The Worker-kill recording below, plus the test pair that kills the Worker before and after the first checkpoint.

### 3. A completed result is never acknowledged

**The failure.** This was the first failure I misunderstood. The initial canary appeared to work: the Workflow completed, and the agent produced a valid result. But the coordinating agent never learned that the result was ready.

I had treated successful notification delivery as successful receipt. Mail and session nudges can wake an agent, but they cannot prove that it reviewed and acknowledged a specific result.

**What Temporal contributes.** Each verified result now creates an <TermTip term="outcome-ready" tip="The durable record that a finished result is owed to someone. It stays open, and delivery repeats, until an acknowledgement matching that exact outcome closes it.">OutcomeReady</TermTip> outbox record and a dedicated Workflow. The Workflow sends a notification, waits durably for acknowledgement, and redelivers while the result remains pending.

**What remains the application's job.** The Workflow closes only when it receives an acknowledgement bound to the exact store, work item, outcome, generation, and session fence. A queued notification or accepted signal proves only that delivery was attempted. The canonical acknowledgement receipt proves that the intended agent accepted the intended result.

**The evidence.** This path failed its first canary, was repaired, and now runs continuously in production. The next two sections explain the failure and the production path.

## What I built

"I added Temporal to my agent system" is too vague to evaluate. The procedure I made durable was narrower: take one ready work item, assign it to one agent session, and accept one generation-fenced receipt.

Before Temporal, that procedure had no single owner. Most of it lived in <TermTip term="bead-reconcile-tick" tip="The old orchestrator's loop step: every few seconds it woke (one tick), scanned the store for ready work, claimed it, and launched agents. Everything it knew between steps lived in process memory.">CityRuntime.beadReconcileTick</TermTip>, with session recovery and completion handled elsewhere. A single controller tick could load state, release orphaned assignments, reconcile active sessions, dispatch notifications, and run a secondary wake-up check. A separate close path searched for work abandoned by dead sessions.

Each part was reasonable in isolation. Together, however, they formed one procedure spread across multiple code paths and persistence boundaries. Every handoff between them created a window in which the process could die after performing an action but before recording what had happened.

### What the tests hold down

The full suite runs with one command (`cd services/temporal-maintenance && make test`, which is `go test -race ./...`). Five invariants carry the design:

1. Duplicate delivery of the same ready event schedules one Activity, not two.
2. A retry attaches to the existing agent session instead of starting a competitor.
3. Stale completions and stale acknowledgements fail closed.
4. Cancellation reaches the exact session that owns the claim, without hanging the Workflow.
5. Captured Workflow histories still replay, and a deliberately planted nondeterministic Workflow fails the replay gate.

The failing replay test matters more than the passing one. A replay gate that never rejects anything may not be wired into the code path it claims to protect. One local rule follows from that: replay fixtures are never regenerated merely to make a change pass. If a captured history stops replaying, the change is breaking until someone can explain why it is safe. The [complete test matrix](/temporal-agent-orchestration/code), with every named case, lives with the annotated source.

Tests still provide weaker evidence than watching the process fail. The same repository includes a harness that runs the real Workflow and Activity against a local Temporal server and kills the Worker with a real operating-system signal, at two points: after the agent is running and a heartbeat checkpoint exists, and before the first heartbeat has been recorded.

Both runs finish with one agent session and one accepted receipt. The second case is the more important one. In the first, the retry can recover the session identity from the heartbeat details. In the second, no checkpoint exists: the retried Activity must call the resolver again, and the resolver must return the session that is already running rather than create another one. That is the test of duplicate prevention. The recording lasts 41 seconds, and most of that is the deliberate pause while Temporal detects that the Worker has disappeared and schedules another attempt. The recovery delay, the repeated resolution, and the single surviving session are stronger evidence than a green test name.

The demo runs on one host against a local dev server, with a fixture process standing in for the coding agent: real Temporal, a real kill signal, a fenced claim. That is enough to justify bounded canaries while canonical mutation stays behind shadow mode; it is not evidence for unrestricted rollout. The [companion page](/temporal-agent-orchestration/) pairs this recording with the same kill against the pre-Temporal orchestrator, which ends with two agents in one worktree and a stale receipt written over the current one.

## The canary failed, but that's why we use canaries

The result-delivery path didn't land cleanly. Its first full-integration canary hit a failed promotion gate and exposed four real defects:

* concurrent notifications contended on a shared mail store;
* correlation metadata named a Workflow that did not exist;
* an observer confused a completed formula step with its still-running source task;
* legitimate completion paths closed real work without producing an <TermTip term="outcome-ready-envelope" tip="The durable record that a finished result is owed to someone. It stays open, and delivery repeats, until an acknowledgement matching that exact outcome closes it.">OutcomeReady</TermTip> envelope at all.

We (mostly the coordinating agent, a.k.a. the Mayor) rolled the integration back, repaired the delivery path, and ran a bounded recovery. The stranded results were delivered and acknowledged.

This was annoying, but it's also why I put in explicit canaries and promotion gates, so at least it did its job. Temporal did not make the first implementation magically correct. It made the procedure observable enough that failures became specific, recoverable, and difficult to silently wave away.

## What's running now

The safest part of the integration is currently running continuously: durable delivery and acknowledgement of finished results. Temporal-driven agent mutation remains in shadow mode. Finished outcomes can be delivered, but Temporal can't yet claim production work or start a coding agent without an explicitly bounded canary.

<ConceptFigure kind="shadow" />

The live result path:

1. Work reaches an end state in Beads, and an <TermTip term="outcome-ready-record" tip="The durable record that a finished result is owed to someone. It stays open, and delivery repeats, until an acknowledgement matching that exact outcome closes it.">OutcomeReady</TermTip> outbox record is written against that work item. When Temporal closes the work itself, the close and the record are one transaction; for the completion paths Temporal doesn't own yet, a reconciler notices the end state afterward and writes the record then.
2. A stable Workflow starts for that result, and a notification Activity tells the current coordinating agent that evidence is available.
3. The Workflow waits durably and redelivers while acknowledgement is missing.
4. The coordinator or a human verifies the evidence and records a disposition bound to the current generation and agent session.
5. Beads stores the exact acknowledgement receipt, which is what allows the Workflow to complete.

Results remain pending until that final receipt exists. The reconciler lane is a reconciliation boundary, not a same-transaction guarantee, which is why an independent watchdog also scans every store for finished or verified work with no outcome, and for store-read failures. The watchdog deliberately sits outside Temporal so that it can detect a silent gap in the system it is checking.

## What Temporal did not solve, and what it cost

Temporal gives a procedure a durable owner. It does not make external effects happen exactly once.

A Worker can die after an external API call succeeds but before Temporal records the Activity completion. Temporal may then run the Activity again. Generation fences make repeated Beads writes safe, and start-or-attach semantics prevent retries from launching duplicate agent sessions. But if an Activity posts to Slack ([I set my agents up to do that](https://github.com/gastownhall/gascity-packs/tree/main/slack-full)), creates a GitHub issue, or calls another external system, safe repetition still depends on that system's idempotency model.

Temporal also does not determine whether the procedure is correct. It does not repair a corrupt Beads store, clean abandoned worktrees, manage disk space, choose approval policies, or make an unsafe external action safe. It does not replace acceptance criteria, deterministic tests, independent review, or human judgment. It remembers the procedure the application defined. The application still has to define a good one.

It preserves mistakes as faithfully as correct behavior. In one canary, the agent completed its work correctly, but an adapter placed the wrong store identity in the outcome envelope. Temporal retried the same invalid envelope every fifteen minutes, exactly as instructed, until I rolled the canary back. Durable execution made the failure persistent and observable rather than silent. It could not correct the adapter's mistake.

The engineering costs were substantial:

* **Workflow changes require replay discipline.** Workflow code must remain deterministic, and previously recorded histories must continue to replay after the code changes. Every orchestration change therefore needs compatibility planning and replay tests.
* **Activities require explicit retry safety.** Stable identities, idempotency keys, claim tokens, generation fences, and start-or-attach behavior remain application responsibilities.
* **Workflow history must stay small.** Prompts, transcripts, and worktree contents cannot be stored directly in Workflow events. Histories contain compact identifiers, fences, hashes, and references to external artifacts.
* **The system gains more infrastructure.** Temporal adds a server, Workers, monitoring, and rollout machinery such as shadow mode, bounded canaries, promotion gates, and rollback paths.
* **Operators must reason about two durable records.** Beads is authoritative for work facts. Temporal Event History is authoritative for procedural progress. Debugging requires knowing which system answers which question.
* **Retries amplify bad instructions too.** Temporal will reliably repeat an incorrect decision until the application changes the decision or stops the execution.

Not every job justified those costs. One maintenance task performs about forty-four seconds of synchronous work every two hours. If it fails, the next scheduled run can safely try again. It remained cron plus a lock because a crash does not leave behind an in-flight procedure that the database cannot explain.

## The decision rule

The pattern that transfers to anyone:

* A Workflow owns durable procedure.
* An Activity owns nondeterministic work.
* Stable identity makes a retry attach to the same logical operation instead of starting a new one.
* External effects stay the application's responsibility.

My additional hardening (generations and claim tokens, session attachment, exact coordinator acknowledgement, the independent watchdog) exists because of what my Activity starts: a process that can edit a Git worktree for an hour and continue running after the Worker that launched it has died. A short, idempotent job needs the first set of guarantees and none of the second.

Use NDI where any route to an acceptable outcome is valid; that is often the right model for the coding work itself. Use deterministic durable replay where the procedure is part of the promise: ordering, retries, waits, acknowledgements, cancellation, and gates. A single multi-agent system can, and arguably should, use both. Gas City supplies the orchestration primitives, Beads supplies the durable work record, and Temporal supplies durable procedure; a future Gas City pack could make that last part something an installation adopts only at the boundaries where its guarantees earn their cost.

> Use Temporal when losing the in-flight procedure would leave the system unable to say what happened.

I still expect the city to have problems. It is a work in progress, and software factories are complex systems running other complex systems. Durability is necessary for reliability, but it is not sufficient. Agents can still make bad decisions, adapters can still carry the wrong identity, and external systems can still fail in ways Temporal cannot fix.

The boundary I want is between freedom in the work and discipline in the machinery around it: let the agents be weird, but make the infrastructure keep its promises.
