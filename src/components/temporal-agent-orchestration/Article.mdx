# I put Temporal around my coding agents. Here's what it fixed, and what it didn't.

*What I learned adding durable execution to a multi-agent coding system built on Beads*

In the fall of 2025, I sat next to Steve Yegge at dinner during a Sourcegraph offsite in Cabo.

Steve had brought his laptop to the table, because of course he had (I was running agents from my phone, so while more discreet, I couldn't judge). Roughly eight terminal windows were stacked across the screen, each in a different color, and he kept moving between them while everyone else was doing more normal dinner activities.

I asked what he was building. I remember the answer being something along the lines of, "Something that's going to be big."

This was believable because it was Steve, but I still had no idea what I was looking at.

A few weeks later, in October 2025, he published [Introducing Beads](https://steve-yegge.medium.com/introducing-beads-a-coding-agent-memory-system-637d7d92514a), a task and dependency system designed around how coding agents actually work.

So the thing on the laptop at dinner really did become big. It was Beads.

## Beads gave my agents a memory outside the chat

Every new coding-agent session starts with some degree of amnesia.

The previous agent may have investigated the codebase, discovered three hidden dependencies, ruled out two approaches, found a broken test, and created a reasonable plan. Then its context fills up or the session ends, and the next agent wakes up knowing none of it.

You can try to solve this with Markdown plans. I certainly did. But then the agent has to keep the plan current, distinguish durable findings from temporary notes, understand which tasks depend on which other tasks, and avoid creating a whole ecosystem of slightly different planning documents.

Beads gives agents a dependency-aware work record instead.

An agent can record what it discovered, connect related work, mark blockers, ask which tasks are ready, and return in another session without reconstructing the entire project from a transcript. Just as importantly, agents seem to understand how to use it. They file issues for problems they encounter and pick up the next unblocked task without much ceremony.

I adopted Beads quickly. It solved a real problem I already had: maintaining task information across sessions without asking each new agent to rediscover the project.

Then Steve built something much larger on top of it.

## Gas Town tried to turn many coding agents into a system

On January 1, 2026, Steve published [Welcome to Gas Town](https://steve-yegge.medium.com/welcome-to-gas-town-4f25ee16dd04).

Gas Town was an opinionated coding-agent factory built on Beads. It added persistent agent identities, disposable sessions, reusable workflows called formulas, coordination through mail and nudges, and a Mayor agent that acted as the top-level coordinator and human handoff point.

It also came with Polecats, a Deacon, a Witness, a Refinery, Rigs, Convoys, Molecules, Wisps, and enough Mad Max terminology that I understood each concept slightly less after learning the name of the next one.

I installed it and dabbled for a bit. I could see the direction immediately: coding agents were becoming capable enough that manually operating each terminal was going to become the bottleneck. The next layer had to coordinate them. At the time I was managing 10+ Ghostty tabs.

But I never quite got my first town working the way I wanted. I had a critical project I was working on at the time ([CodeScaleBench](https://github.com/sourcegraph/CodeScaleBench)) that I couldn't really afford to stall on dropped queues and other random orchestrator nonsense. Gas Town expected a fair amount of operational context, and I was still trying to understand how all the parts related to one another. Eventually I backed out.

I could run a bunch of coding agents, but I didn't know how to keep a town of them from catching fire.

While thinking about this whole town, orchestration, whatever you want to call it philosophy of Gas Town, in the back of my mind I was grappling with what he called in his post **Nondeterministic Idempotence**, or NDI.

The basic idea is that the exact path through the work does not need to be reproducible. A session can crash. A new agent can inspect the durable work record (Beads store), infer what happened, and take a different route forward. As long as agents keep consulting the work record and eventually satisfy the acceptance criteria, the workflow can recover.

This is a good fit for coding agents because coding itself is nondeterministic. Two agents can solve the same issue in very different ways, and either result may be acceptable (unless you're a [SWE-Bench verifier](https://openai.com/index/why-we-no-longer-evaluate-swe-bench-verified/)).

Gas Town sought to make the work durable without trying to make the agent deterministic.

## And then came Gas City

Around March, before its public launch, I found the project that became [Gas City](https://github.com/gastownhall/gascity).

Gas City, created by Julian Knutsen and Chris Sells, took the primitives behind Gas Town and turned them into a composable orchestration SDK. Gas Town was one specific city design. Gas City gave you the building materials to create your own.

That separation immediately made sense to me and I was (and still am) super bought in.

Instead of adopting one fixed hierarchy of roles, I could decide which agents existed, how work moved between them, which workflows should run, what evidence reviewers required, and where humans entered the loop. I could keep the useful substrate from Gas Town without reproducing every part of the original town.

I began contributing heavily, eventually handling [over two hundred pull requests](https://github.com/gastownhall/gascity/pulls?q=is%3Apr+author%3Asjarmak+is%3Amerged) across session lifecycle, reconciliation, Beads integration, formulas, dispatch, the API, repair controls, and external messaging. Julian invited me to become Gas City's first outside maintainer.

I now use Gas City to run work across my (many, too many) projects, including this website, and Gas City itself. It is a strange but useful test environment: the city builds and repairs the software that operates the city.

A system that uses itself is also extremely good at finding orchestration bugs.

## What running a real city exposed

A simplified Gas City setup looks something like this:

* **Beads** records the work, dependencies, claims, artifacts, and outcomes.
* **Rigs** isolate projects and their worker pools.
* **Agent templates** define reusable worker roles.
* **Named sessions** give running agents stable addresses.
* **Formulas** describe linked steps and dependencies.
* **Dispatchers and hooks** assign eligible work and wake sessions.
* **Reviews and gates** record whether the work was accepted.
* **Sweeps and health patrol** inspect stale claims, dead sessions, branches, and handoffs.

My installation also adds coordinating roles. Each project has a Project Lead responsible for evidence, gates, and handoffs. A Mayor coordinates work across the broader city.

<ConceptFigure kind="orchestrator" />

This architecture can produce a lot of useful work. It can also produce wonderfully complicated failure modes.

For example...A work item becomes ready. The Mayor claims it and launches a coding agent. The agent gets a worktree, edits files, runs tests, and works for an hour.

Then the Mayor's session dies before it records the handoff.

What should happen next?

The system could launch another agent and accidentally leave two processes editing the same worktree. It could wait forever for a process it no longer knows how to contact. It could release the claim and ask a repair loop to infer what happened from whatever evidence the first process left behind.

At various points, I have operated versions of all three.

The relevant Beads usually survived. The facts about the task were still there. But the procedure existed as a collection of clues spread across controller ticks, session records, mail, hooks, process state, and repair logic.

<ConspiracyFigure />

At enough scale, those clues become increasingly useless.

I saw stranded claims, duplicate sessions after retries, stale completions arriving after a newer generation had started, lost notifications, correlation metadata pointing at executions that did not exist, formula steps confused with their source tasks, and maintenance cycles capable of reporting failure without owning a complete recovery path.

The problem was not that Beads had failed to preserve the work. It had done that part. It was super durable in that the work record was still around.

The problem was that my many agents and I had hand-rolled a distributed procedure without giving that procedure one durable owner.

## NDI isn't the issue

It would be easy to frame this as "nondeterminism is bad, so make everything deterministic," but that's not the solution, we obviously need nondeterminism in the form of the agent's individual actions that culminate in produced work. But where do we draw the line?

NDI is useful when any reasonable path to an acceptable outcome is valid, which describes a lot of agent work.

An agent can inspect a bug, choose a repair, run tests, realize it was wrong, backtrack, and try something else. I do not need a workflow engine to replay every thought or tool call in exactly the same order. I actively do not want that.

But some parts of an orchestration system are promises rather than work:

* Did this claim already start an agent?
* Does this result belong to the current generation?
* Has this exact outcome been acknowledged?
* Is a retry resuming the same execution or creating a competing one?
* Which recovery action owns the next transition?
* Should a late completion still be allowed to mutate the work record?

Those are procedural questions. "A capable agent will inspect the clues and figure something out" is not always the guarantee I want around them. Turns out they aren't that capable or durable.

The flaw I was dealing with was asking an NDI-style recovery model to own guarantees whose ordering, retries, waits, identities, and acknowledgements were themselves part of the contract.

For those parts, I need a little more determinism.

## I had already been studying the system around the agent

At the same time, I had been researching how coding agents fail and what it takes to run them reliably.

[Agent Diagnostics](https://github.com/sjarmak/agent-diagnostics) classifies why coding-agent runs succeed or fail rather than reducing everything to one pass/fail score. [CodeScaleBench](https://github.com/sourcegraph/CodeScaleBench) tests agents on large repositories with deterministic verifiers and records the retrieval and execution traces behind the result.

In June, I gave a Sourcegraph webinar on [how to run coding agents reliably in large codebases](https://sourcegraph.com/webinars/building-a-software-factory).

The recurring lesson from all of that work was that reliability is complex. Retrieval, task state, tool behavior, review, recovery, and the rest of the system around the model often decide whether a long-running agent can be trusted.

I was studying those questions at Sourcegraph, but I also had a very practical open-source motivation: I reeeeally wanted Gas City to just work. I wanted it to be foolproof for fools like me and to not have to deal with murder scene detective work all the time.

You really shouldn't need a perfect mental model of every controller race and recovery path to keep a city of agents alive. I definitely do not want to be the person who does, poking the agents, trying to determine whether the agent that appears dead is actually dead, temporarily unreachable, or still editing a branch that a second agent has now claimed.

So I started looking for systems that had already solved adjacent problems.

## Kubernetes could restart the process, but not the promise

Because Gas Town is often described as "Kubernetes for agents," I spent time looking at Kubernetes controllers, reconciliation loops, leases, ownership, and process placement. I picked up a [Designing Distributed Systems](https://www.oreilly.com/library/view/designing-distributed-systems/9781491983638/) book for funsies.

Some of the ideas were useful. Gas City already has a controller that compares desired state with observed state and tries to reconcile the difference.

But Kubernetes, even just conceptually, was not quite the missing piece for my durability needs.

Kubernetes can determine that a process disappeared and start another one. It does not inherently know whether a particular work claim already launched this particular coding-agent session, whether a late result belongs to the current claim generation, or whether a human acknowledged the exact outcome the system is waiting for.

The broader distributed-systems toolbox gave me many of the necessary pieces:

* transactional outboxes;
* stable identities;
* idempotency keys;
* generation fencing;
* explicit acknowledgements;
* leases and heartbeats;
* retry-safe adapters.

What I still lacked was one durable owner for the sequence connecting those pieces.

Then I remembered Steve saying Gas Town looked like what you'd get if Kubernetes and [Temporal](https://docs.temporal.io/temporal) had a very ugly baby together, so that was some additional heritage to look into.

## How is Gas Town like Temporal?

Steve described NDI as similar to Temporal's deterministic durable replay, but implemented through completely different machinery. He also explicitly said Gas Town was not a replacement for Temporal.

Gas Town had chosen a model where persistent agents inspect persistent work and infer a viable continuation.

Temporal uses a different model. A [Workflow](https://docs.temporal.io/workflows) records a deterministic procedure in an [Event History](https://docs.temporal.io/encyclopedia/event-history). When a [Worker](https://docs.temporal.io/workers) crashes, another Worker can replay that history, reconstruct the Workflow's state, and continue from the recorded boundary.

External and nondeterministic effects run in [Activities](https://docs.temporal.io/activities).

This raised the question that became the project:

**Could the setup I run with Gas City use both models at once?**

I did not want to put the coding agent's reasoning inside deterministic replay. That would be both impractical and just plain wrong.

I wanted to keep the agent nondeterministic while making selected promises around it durable.

The boundary I arrived at was:

> Put the unpredictable agent inside an Activity. Put the promises around it in a Workflow.

## Adding Temporal to my city was mostly an exercise in deciding who owned what

At first I thought I was adding a workflow engine.

What I was actually doing was forcing myself to answer a series of ownership questions that the existing system had been allowed to leave fuzzy.

Before Temporal, one controller tick could load snapshots, release orphaned assignments, reconcile sessions, dispatch notifications, and run a second wake-up backstop. A separate close path searched for work left behind by dead sessions.

Each individual piece kinda made sense, but the complete procedure was distributed across several pieces of code and several persistence boundaries.

But...Every arrow between them was a place where the process could die.

<AnnotatedCode mode="before" />

The integration eventually settled on three owners:

### Beads owns the work record

In my setup, the Beads store (running on a [Dolt](https://github.com/dolthub/dolt) server) remains the canonical record for mutable work facts:

* tasks and dependencies;
* claims;
* generations;
* artifacts;
* evidence;
* outcomes;
* acknowledgement receipts.

Temporal does not replace Beads. A user or agent should still be able to inspect the work independently of Temporal.

### Temporal owns durable procedure

Temporal Workflow History records which orchestration steps occurred, which retry is active, what the system is waiting for, and what may safely happen next.

It owns waits, retries, cancellation, signals, and procedure-level progress.

### Activities contain nondeterministic effects

Agent processes, tests, reviews, database calls, external messages, and other side effects run inside Activities.

The coding agent remains unpredictable. Temporal records that the Activity was scheduled, tracks its execution, receives heartbeats, and decides what to do if the Worker disappears.

<ConceptFigure kind="ownership" />

You can think of the system as:

Gas City is the workshop. Beads is the job board. It records the work, who owns it, and what happened.

Coding agents are the workers. Their path through the job may be creative, messy, or horribly weird.

Temporal is the checklist recorder. It remembers which procedural steps happened, which step is waiting, and which safe retry comes next after some inevitable mishap (complex systems have emergent behaviors, some of which are really annoying and destructive!).

## The three failures I wanted Temporal to stop

The implementation focused on three concrete breakpoints. The first two are built and proved by a bounded canary, but still run in shadow mode; only the third runs continuously today.

### 1. A crash between claiming work and launching the agent

Previously, the system could record that work was ready and then die while trying to deliver the event that launched its execution.

Now the ready transition and its delivery event are written together in one Beads transaction. A bridge reads the pending event and uses Temporal's [Signal-With-Start](https://docs.temporal.io/develop/go/workflows/message-passing#signal-with-start) operation with a stable [Workflow ID](https://docs.temporal.io/workflow-execution/workflowid-runid) derived from the city, run, and work item.

If a Workflow with that ID is already running, the event is signaled to it. Otherwise, Temporal starts it and delivers the signal as part of the same operation.

There is still an unavoidable crash window: Temporal can accept the event, then the bridge can die before acknowledging the outbox record.

The fix is not pretending that window disappeared. The event is delivered again, and the Workflow ignores an event ID it has already processed (so, deterministic idempotency? I guess we just call that idempotency?).

Maintaining a stable identity turns redelivery from a dangerous anomaly into normal, expected behavior that doesn't break the system.

<ConceptFigure kind="handoff" />

### 2. A retry accidentally starts a second agent

Temporal Activities may execute more than once. A Worker can die after an external effect succeeds but before Temporal records its completion.

That means "Temporal will retry it" is not, by itself, safe for starting coding agents. A naïve retry could launch a second agent against the same work.

The Activity now claims one exact work generation and receives a fencing token. A trusted adapter uses that token to resolve the agent session. It either starts the session once or returns the session already bound to that claim.

The Activity [heartbeats](https://docs.temporal.io/develop/go/activities/timeouts#activity-heartbeats) the binding and execution progress.

If the Worker disappears, Temporal can schedule another attempt. The replacement reads the persisted heartbeat details, presents the same claim token, and reattaches to the existing session rather than launching a competitor.

Completion also goes through the fence. Beads accepts a receipt only when the work ID, generation, claim token, and the claiming Workflow identity still match.

A stale agent can complete successfully and still be rejected because it no longer owns the right to mutate the current work generation.

<ConceptFigure kind="recovery" />

### 3. A completed result never reaches anyone

This was the failure I got wrong first.

An initial bead-to-agent canary I set up worked. The Workflow completed correctly and the agent produced a valid result.

Buuuuut, the Mayor never learned that the result existed.

I had treated mail and session nudges as if successful delivery meant successful receipt (nope). A best-effort notification can wake an agent up, but it can't prove that the exact result was reviewed and acknowledged.

So each verified result now gets an `OutcomeReady` outbox record and its own Temporal Workflow.

That Workflow:

1. delivers a notification;
2. waits durably;
3. redelivers while the result remains pending;
4. closes only after receiving an acknowledgement bound to the exact store, work item, outcome, generation, and session fence.

Queued notifications and accepted signals are evidence that delivery was attempted.

Only the canonical acknowledgement receipt closes the loop.

<ConceptFigure kind="workshop" />

## The canary failed, but that's why we use canaries

The result-delivery path didn't end up landing cleanly.

Its first full-integration canary hit a failed promotion gate and exposed four real defects:

* concurrent notifications contended on a shared mail store;
* correlation metadata named a Workflow that did not exist;
* an observer confused a completed formula step with its still-running source task;
* legitimate completion paths closed real work without producing an `OutcomeReady` envelope at all.

<CanaryFigure />

We (mostly the Mayor) rolled the integration back, repaired the delivery path, and ran a bounded recovery. The stranded results were delivered and acknowledged.

This was annoying, but it's also why I put in explicit canaries and promotion gates, so at least it did its job.

Temporal did not make the first implementation magically correct. It made the procedure observable enough that failures became specific, recoverable, and difficult to silently wave away.

## What's running now

The safest part of the integration is currently running continuously: durable delivery and acknowledgement of finished results.

Temporal-driven agent mutation remains in shadow mode. In the service configuration, finished outcomes can be delivered, but Temporal can't yet claim production work or start a coding agent without an explicitly bounded canary.

The live result path is:

1. Work reaches an end state in Beads.
2. An `OutcomeReady` outbox record is written against that work item. When Temporal closes the work itself, the close and the record are one transaction. For the completion paths Temporal doesn't own yet, a reconciler notices the end state afterward and writes the record then.
3. A stable Temporal Workflow starts for that result.
4. A notification Activity tells the current Mayor that evidence is available.
5. The Workflow waits durably and redelivers while acknowledgement is missing.
6. The Mayor or a human verifies the evidence and records a disposition bound to the current generation and agent session.
7. Beads stores the exact acknowledgement receipt, allowing the Workflow to complete.

Results remain pending until that final receipt exists.

That second lane is a reconciliation boundary, not a same-transaction guarantee. It's the reason the watchdog exists.

An independent watchdog also scans for finished or verified work with no outcome and for store-read failures. The watchdog deliberately sits outside Temporal so that it can detect a silent gap in the system it is checking.

## What Temporal did not solve

Temporal gives the procedure a durable owner. It does not make external effects exactly once.

A Worker can still die after an external API call succeeds but before Temporal records the Activity completion. The Activity may then run again.

Generation fences protect Beads writes. Start-or-attach semantics protect agent sessions. But if an Activity sends a message to Slack (I set my agents up to do that), creates a GitHub issue, or calls some other external destination, exactly-once behavior still depends on that destination's idempotency model.

Temporal also does not decide whether code is correct.

It does not repair a corrupt Beads store, clean abandoned worktrees, manage disk space, choose appropriate approval policies, or make an unsafe external action safe. It does not replace acceptance criteria, deterministic tests, independent review, or human judgment.

It remembers the procedure but the application still has to define a good one.

## NDI and durable replay are complementary

I initially approached this as a choice between two orchestration philosophies, but that's not really what it is.

Use NDI where any route to an acceptable outcome is valid. That is often the right model for the coding work itself.

Use deterministic durable replay where the procedure is part of the promise: ordering, retries, waits, acknowledgements, cancellation, and gates.

A single multi-agent system can (and arguably, should) use both.

The work record can remain inspectable and flexible. Agents can continue making nondeterministic decisions. Temporal Workflows can own the small number of procedures where losing the exact state transition creates dangerous ambiguity.

Gas City supplies the orchestration primitives. Beads supplies the durable work record. Temporal supplies durable procedure.

## Toward a Temporal pack

The implementation is a standalone Go service using Gas City's canonical work store and local command surfaces. It's not something I needed to wire in as a core dependency for Gas City (i.e., the SDK repo) itself.

The eventual distribution boundary could be a Gas City pack containing the Worker configuration, Workflow and Activity contracts, Beads adapters, generation fences, outcome-delivery tools, canary and rollback gates, diagnostics, and user runbooks.

An installation could then choose Temporal only at the workflow boundaries where its guarantees are useful.

That optionality is important; I don't think every formula needs to become a Temporal Workflow. Doing so would add machinery without necessarily adding value.

You can find the relevant boundaries by asking which guarantee needs to survive a crash.

Sometimes the answer is only the work record, and in that case Beads is enough.

Sometimes the answer is that an agent eventually finds some acceptable route to completion. NDI is a good model.

Sometimes the answer includes the exact sequence of waits, retries, identities, gates, and acknowledgements. That procedure needs a durable owner.

## What I learned

I did not add Temporal to make coding agents deterministic. Their ability to explore, backtrack, and solve problems in ways I did not precompute is the whole reason to use them.

I added it because I think some parts of the system really should not be improvising.

I still expect the city to catch fire sometimes. Complex systems do, and agents surprise us all daily. What I do not want is for every fire to turn into a murder mystery where a new agent has to reconstruct the control plane from stale claims, half-delivered messages, and whatever process happens to still be alive.

The agents can take whatever path they need inside the boundary. Beads can preserve what they learn. Temporal can remember which promises are still outstanding and what must happen next.

That feels like the right split for the systems I want to build: let the agents be weird, but make the infrastructure keep its promises.
